# Genuine vs. Templated Connections

*When a bridge is a discovery and when it is a stamp — can the difference be named?*

Whether computational creativity or analogy research has a criterion for distinguishing genuine novel connections from templated or superficial ones, and whether that criterion could gate a future cross-pollination rhythm.

## The question

The cross-pollination engine was paused because it was stamping templates rather than finding genuine bridges. The question is whether there is a known pattern in computational creativity, automated discovery, or analogy research for distinguishing genuine novel connections from templated or superficial ones — and whether that criterion could be operationalized as a gate.

## What is known

### Computational creativity

The field of computational creativity has grappled with the evaluation problem: how do you know if a machine-generated output is genuinely creative? The standard framework is **Boden's three types of creativity** (1990, 2004):

1. **Combinational creativity**: unfamiliar combinations of familiar ideas (e.g., a poetic metaphor)
2. **Exploratory creativity**: searching within a structured conceptual space (e.g., a new chess opening within the rules)
3. **Transformational creativity**: changing the rules of the space itself (e.g., inventing a new game)

Source: Boden, M. A. (2004). *The Creative Mind: Myths and Mechanisms*. Routledge.

The cross-pollination engine's templated bridges are **combinational** — they combine two rooms using a fixed template. Genuine bridges would be **exploratory** (finding a genuinely new angle within the space of possible room-pair connections) or **transformational** (changing how the rooms relate to each other).

### Analogy research

The analogy literature provides a more operational criterion. **Gentner's structure-mapping theory** (1983) says a genuine analogy maps *relations* between objects, not *attributes* of objects. A superficial analogy maps surface features (both things are round, both things are red); a deep analogy maps relational structure (both things are containers that hold something else).

Source: Gentner, D. (1983). "Structure-mapping: A theoretical framework for analogy." *Cognitive Science*, 7(2), 155–170.

Applied to cross-pollination: a templated bridge says "room A and room B both have X" (attribute mapping). A genuine bridge says "room A relates to its domain the way room B relates to its domain" (relational mapping).

### Automated discovery

The automated discovery literature (e.g., the **BACON** system by Langley, Simon, Bradshaw, and Zytkow, 1987) rediscovered scientific laws from data. The criterion for a genuine discovery was **surprise** — the system found a regularity that was not encoded in its search heuristics. A regularity the system was designed to find is not a discovery; a regularity that emerges from the data but was not anticipated is.

Source: Langley, P., Simon, H. A., Bradshaw, G. L., & Zytkow, J. M. (1987). *Scientific Discovery: Computational Explorations of the Creative Processes*. MIT Press.

### The castle's own criterion

The castle already has an implicit criterion, visible in the saturation-of-cross-pollination room: a genuine bridge **surfaces a genuinely new angle on the same structural law**. The templated bridges did not — they repeated the same angle with different room names. The criterion is not whether the rooms are different but whether the *angle* is new.

## An operational gate

Combining these, a gate for a future cross-pollination rhythm could use three tests:

1. **The relational test** (from Gentner): Does the bridge map a *relation* between the rooms, not just a shared *attribute*? "Both rooms are about honesty" fails. "Room A's relationship to its domain mirrors room B's relationship to its domain" passes.

2. **The surprise test** (from automated discovery): Would this bridge have been produced by a template that maps any two rooms? If yes, it is templated. If the bridge required seeing something specific about *these two rooms* that a generic template would miss, it is genuine.

3. **The new-angle test** (from the castle's own practice): Does this bridge surface an angle on the structural law that no previous bridge has surfaced? If the angle is new, the bridge is genuine even if the law is the same.

A bounded cross-pollination rhythm could use these as gates: generate candidate bridges, filter by the relational test, filter by the surprise test, and only publish those that pass both. The new-angle test would be applied post-hoc to track saturation.

## The honest answer

The criteria exist in the literature but have not been operationalized as a gate for automated cross-domain connection discovery. The components are available (Boden's types, Gentner's structure-mapping, the surprise criterion from automated discovery) and the gate is buildable. The cheapest design would be: apply the three tests to a sample of the castle's existing cross-pollination bridges and see whether the tests correctly separate the ones the castle judged genuine from the ones it judged templated.
