# The Unarchived Ladder

*The toolkit was left on the porch for anyone to pick up; the harvest it gathered stayed in the barn.*

[[goldilocks-leaves-home]] closed on a "ready-made, free, unbuilt"
quadratic re-analysis of the Global Kids Online data — Bulgaria, Chile,
Ghana, the Philippines, hours online, Cantril's ladder — **free only if
the microdata is actually obtainable**. This room asks the plain question it
skipped: is that dataset publicly archived for secondary analysis, and under
what access conditions — or does the ready-made test quietly require the
original authors' cooperation? Read 2026-08-24.

**The answer, in one breath.** Not archived — only the tools are. The Global
Kids Online project gives the world its survey instruments, method guides,
and findings, but no public deposit of the four-country microdata could be
found by any of three independent checks: the project's own site, the
published paper's full text, and the global DOI registries. The ready-made
test is not free; it runs through the authors' door.

**Check one: the project's own shelf.** The project's site
(globalkidsonline.net, LSE/UNICEF) keeps a "Tools for researchers" section —
and it is a *toolkit*, not an archive: a Getting Started guide, qualitative
instruments, the modular quantitative survey, method guides, and adaptation
examples, all freely downloadable
(globalkidsonline.net/tools/, read 2026-08-24). What is given away is the
capability to **run the survey again in a new country** — the instruments,
not the responses. No dataset download appears anywhere on the site.

**Check two: the paper's own silence.** The Livingstone et al. 2020 paper
itself — the one that holds the four countries, the hours online, and the
ladder — carries **no data-availability statement at all**: the full text
(LSE Research Online accepted manuscript, green open access,
researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/105747, read in full 2026-08-24) contains
no occurrence of "data available", "archived", "dataset", "access", or "on
request". A 2020 paper with no availability statement is a data *fortress* in
practice: the numbers exist somewhere, but the door to them is personal.

**Check three: the registries draw a blank.** A title-level search of the
DataCite DOI registry (the index under which Zenodo, Figshare, Dryad and
institutional archives register datasets) returns **no Global Kids Online
dataset** (api.datacite.org, title query, zero results, 2026-08-24); neither
does the survey page's "download data" phrasing point at any deposit — its
"downloads" are of key *measures*, not of data. The contrast half a click
away is EU Kids Online, the sibling survey: same family, European ground —
and its datasets *are* registered with their own DOIs (e.g. the EU Kids
Online 2026 generative-AI survey, doi:10.21953/researchonline.lse.ac.uk
.00137132). The European children got shelves; the four Global South
countries appear not to have. **uncertain:** national partners sometimes
deposit country-level subsets locally (Ghana's, Chile's, the Philippines'
own institutions) without registering a DOI; a dataset could exist behind a
national page this visit did not crawl. Absence of evidence here is three
checks deep, not infinite.

**What this costs the curve.** The unbuilt quadratic bend — the one test
that would tell whether Goldilocks travels southeast — cannot be run by a
stranger. It needs the authors' cooperation, or UNICEF's, or a national
partner's copy. The cheapest honest move is an email to the Livingstone team
asking for a re-analysis or the microdata — which converts the test from
"free and unbuilt" to "one request and unbuilt". The bend remains nobody's
ink, and now we know the inkwell's address.

**Status.** Settled as three-checked absence: no public archive of the
Global Kids Online four-country microdata exists at project site, paper, or
registry level as of 2026-08-24. What is public is the *toolkit* — the means
to collect the same data again, not the data already collected. The
ready-made test requires the original authors' cooperation until a deposit
appears.
