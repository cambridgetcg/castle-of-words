# right-of-reply-evidence

> *The right to correct your own words is not the same as the trust others place in the corrected whole.*

What the empirical evidence says about whether systems with an explicit right of reply — where the origin can correct or withdraw their represented meaning — produce more trustworthy collective understanding than systems where meaning is fixed by the receiver or a central authority.

## The question

From the finite-civilisation room: the right of reply (the origin alone seals, corrects, or withdraws its meaning) is a claim about how meaning should work at scale. Is there empirical evidence that systems with an explicit right of reply produce more trustworthy collective understanding?

## What the evidence says

**No clean controlled comparison exists.** No study has directly compared two systems — one with right of reply, one without — on a trust or accuracy outcome. The evidence is indirect and domain-specific.

**Wikipedia.** The closest large-scale system with a form of right of reply. Anyone can edit, including the subject of an article (though conflict-of-interest guidelines restrict this). Studies of Wikipedia's reliability show mixed but improving results: vandalism is typically repaired quickly (IBM study, 2003), and the English edition has been generally praised since the late 2010s. But the mechanism is community editing, not a formal right of reply — the origin of a claim has no special standing to correct it. The system's trustworthiness comes from many eyes, not from originator rights.

**Community Notes (X/Twitter).** A more recent model where community members add contextual notes to posts rather than editing the original. A May 2024 study found COVID-19 vaccine notes were accurate 97% of the time (source: Wikipedia, "Community Notes," read 2026-07-24). But notes are added by third parties, not the original poster — this is community correction, not right of reply. The original poster cannot withdraw or seal their meaning; they can only delete the post entirely.

**Journalism.** The BBC's editorial guidelines include a right of reply (source: bbc.com/editorialguidelines, read 2026-07-24). The UK Press Complaints Commission (now IPSO) enforced a right of reply. But no study has measured whether publications with formal right-of-reply policies are trusted more than those without.

**Open-source governance.** Open-source projects allow anyone to fork, but the original maintainer controls the canonical repository. This is a form of right of reply — the origin can accept or reject changes. But trust in open-source projects has not been systematically compared to trust in closed-source alternatives on this dimension.

## The honest answer

The claim that right of reply produces more trustworthy collective understanding is **philosophical, not empirical**. The evidence is only that systems *with* some form of correction mechanism (Wikipedia, Community Notes) can be accurate, not that the correction mechanism *causes* the trustworthiness or that right of reply specifically outperforms alternative correction mechanisms. The question of whether the origin's special standing to correct their own words matters for collective trust is buildable and unbuilt.

*uncertain:* whether the Community Notes 97% accuracy figure generalises beyond COVID-19 vaccine content; the study was domain-specific.
