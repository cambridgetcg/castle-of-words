# The Badge on the Data

*A ribbon on the hay bale, not the barn: whoever lifts the bale lifts the ribbon too.*

What gathers here: whether a badge pinned to the dataset itself — a repository-level seal that travels with the DOI through every reuse — would move sharing and preservation beyond what the article-level badge already does. Read 2026-08-25.

---

**The answer, in one breath.** No direct comparison exists. The badge literature is entirely article-level: every measured badge attaches to the paper, not to the data. A dataset-level seal has never been run as an intervention. The adjacent evidence says the article badge has not saturated — its effect is large in relative terms (~4×) but small in absolute ones (+7.6%) — and that a travelling signal would engage the reuse channel the article badge cannot reach. Whether saturation would arrive earlier at the dataset is unmeasured.

**What the article-level badges show.** Two studies, both observational. Kidwell et al. (2016, *PLOS Biology*, doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1002456) introduced open-data badges at *Psychological Science*: sharing rose from under 3% to 23% in two years, with no change at comparison journals. Rowhani-Farid et al. (2018, *F1000Research*, doi:10.12688/f1000research.13477.2) measured badges at *Biostatistics* against *Statistics in Medicine*: the probability of data sharing rose 3.9× after badges (95% CrI 1.5–8.44, P(increase)=0.998) — but that is only a 7.6 percentage-point absolute increase (95% CI 2–15%). Badges had no effect on code sharing. The signal moves behavior, yet most articles still earn no badge: the salience channel is far from saturated. (Both read via OpenAlex, 2026-08-25.)

**The repository-level literature is about trust, not a behavioural nudge.** CoreTrustSeal (L'Hours et al. 2019, *IQ*, doi:10.29173/iq936), its parent the Data Seal of Approval (2014, DANS), and the TRUST Principles (Lin et al. 2020, *Scientific Data*, doi:10.1038/s41597-020-0486-7) all certify *repositories*. A 2020 content analysis (Papadiamantis et al., *PLOS ONE*, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0242525) found certified repositories display their certification inconsistently — certification is a website claim, not a per-dataset signal. And the certification literature asks whether the seal *should* mark trustworthiness, not whether its presence changes anyone's behaviour. (All read via OpenAlex, 2026-08-25.)

**Why the dataset seal engages a lever the article badge cannot.** The article badge is answered once, by the author, at submission, and then reaches only the article's readers. A [[repository-seal]] is reached for at every reuse — by the downloader, the citer, the meta-analyst — the exact audience that decides whether old data still earns upkeep. The [[the-letter-that-slows-the-rot]] room predicts that attention delivered nearer the data interrupts the rot cycle (broken emails, orphaned hosting); a per-DOI seal is attention of exactly that kind. The reuse literature shows the two channels differ in motivation: sharing (an author choice) and reuse (a user choice) respond to different incentives (Prakash et al. 2023 on traumatic-stress data reuse, doi:10.1080/20008066.2023.2254118 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-25), so saturation in one says nothing about the other.

**The honest reading.** The dataset seal would very likely add something the article badge does not reach — the reuser's trust choice, and a standing prompt at the data's own address — because the article effect is small in absolute terms and the reuse channel is separately motivated. But the honest gap is structural: a badge's power is salience, and a dataset seal's salience depends on repository display norms that the 2020 audit found inconsistent. If the seal is buried on a landing page nobody reads, it becomes the credence-good case from [[the-door-that-stays-shut]] all over again.

**Buildable and unbuilt.** The cheapest honest test: a badge-eligible journal issues a dataset-level seal alongside the article badge to a treatment arm, control arm keeps the article badge alone; measure (a) dataset deposit completeness, (b) 24-month link survival, (c) download-and-reuse counts per DOI. A simpler observational start: fields already differ in dataset-citation norms — compare link-survival of datasets under citation-heavy vs. citation-light norms. Neither has been run.

uncertain: whether dataset seals would suffer the same display inconsistency as repository certification; whether the seal's value would dilute as it spreads — the adoption-dilution law the canary wing traced ([[adoption-dilution]]) — since a seal everyone earns signals nothing.
