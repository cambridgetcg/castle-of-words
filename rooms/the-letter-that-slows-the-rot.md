# The Letter That Slows the Rot

*A knock on the barn door is also a hand on the latch: the asking may keep the hay inside.*

What gathers here: whether a data-request letter does more than ask — whether the asking itself is a preservation intervention that slows the decay of the asked-for data. Read 2026-08-24.

---

**The answer, in one breath.** No study has measured the survival-of-the-attended effect: whether a researcher who receives one data request in a year is more likely to still have their data accessible the next. The direct test is buildable and unbuilt. The adjacent evidence says the asking would slow the rot, but by an unknown amount and with an unknown half-life.

**What the unwritten-letter room already established.** [[the-unwritten-letter]] carries the two curves: the response rate of a data-request letter (17%–27%, from Savage & Vickers 2009 and the 2023 *Meta-Psychology* review) and the decay rate of the underlying data (~17% odds-loss per year, from Vines et al. 2014). The room's door asked what the asking is owed; this room asks what the asking *does*.

**What the adjacent literatures predict.** (1) **Incentive and attention effects:** the badge study (Kidwell et al. 2016, *PLOS Biology*, doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1002456 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24) showed that a low-cost signal — the chance to earn an "open data" badge — moved data sharing from under 3% to 23% at *Psychological Science*, with no change in comparison journals. The mechanism was not enforcement but *attention*: the badge made the journal's expectation salient. A letter is a stronger signal than a badge — it names a specific person asking for a specific thing — and would be predicted to produce the same preservation behavior in the recipient. (2) **The decay mechanisms:** Vines et al. found the rot runs through broken email addresses, media obsolescence, and lost institutional hosting — all failures of attention and maintenance. A letter interrupts every one: it prompts the recipient to check the storage, update the contact, or move the files. (3) **The audit tradition:** the library and RDM literature (Federer et al. 2018, *PLOS ONE*, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0194768 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24) found Data Availability Statements durable but untracked longitudinally; the closest analogue is the clinical-trial IPD-sharing literature, where a *request* from the funder or consortium was the trigger for archiving, but no study measured the counterfactual decay of the unasked-for.

**The honest reading.** The asking is almost certainly a preservation intervention. A letter forces the recipient to look at the barn; the looking finds the rot before it finishes. But the size of the effect is unmeasured — it may be a delay of a year or two (the time to fix the email and move the files) or a genuine rescue (the data enters a repository). The letter's cost and its preservation value are on the same ledger: the asker pays the scope and credit, and the recipient pays a smaller cost that saves the data for everyone.

**Buildable and unbuilt.** The test: a cohort of researchers whose data are known to be aging (e.g., from Vines's original evolutionary-biology cohort), half sent a data-request letter, half not, then re-check data accessibility at 6, 18, and 36 months. The design is a standard field experiment with a survival outcome; it has never been run.

uncertain: whether the effect would survive in a field with lower baseline sharing norms, or whether the recipient who cannot find the data simply does not reply, making the response rate itself an underestimate of the preservation effect (the non-responders may be the ones whose data were already lost).
