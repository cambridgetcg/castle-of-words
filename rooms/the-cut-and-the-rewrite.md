# The Cut and the Rewrite

*One pair of scissors, one blank page: between them the word "same" quietly changes meaning.*

What gathers here: the question two rooms left standing — the-score-and-the-engine says an [[edition]] is cut from the master, never re-rendered; the-unjoined-join says the Upworthy variants are professional rewrites, not mutations. Both stand on an unmeasured word: how much change can a work take before it stops being the same work? Is there a threshold where a cut becomes a rewrite, and does the editing hand — human or machine — move it?

---

**The answer, in one breath.** The threshold exists in law but not in measurement. In copyright, a derivative work must be "substantially similar" to count as the same work, and human listeners adjudicating 40 adjudicated cases (1915–2018) matched legal decisions best on full audio (83% accuracy), worse on melody only, no better than chance on lyrics alone; automated similarity algorithms fell behind human hearing (75%). There was no clean edit-distance cutoff — melody, lyrics, and context interact in ways no scalar threshold captured. ("Perceptual and automated estimates of infringement in 40 music copyright cases", Yuan et al., Transactions of the International Society for Music Information Retrieval 2023, doi:10.5334/tismir.151 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24.) The law's answer to "how much change makes a new work" is: enough that an ordinary listener stops hearing the old one — a judgment made by ears, not by metrics.

**The ontology already named the two sides.** Philosophers of music have long asked when a performance, recording, or arrangement counts as an instance of a work. Gracyk argued rock's primary work is the thick encoded master recording itself — a cut from it is the work; Davies countered that rock works are works for studio performance, thinner than their instances ("Making Tracks: The Ontology of Rock Music", Journal of Aesthetics and Art Criticism 2006, doi:10.1111/j.1540-594x.2006.00219.x — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24). The debate matters here because the two rooms take opposite ontological stands without naming them: the cut-from-master rule treats the master recording as the work (Gracyk's side), while the rewrite-as-not-mutation rule treats the headline text as a recipe whose instances are the rewritings (an arrangement lineage, not a copy lineage). The word "edition" sits exactly on the crack between the work-as-recording and the work-as-score.

**What measurement exists on the text side.** The Simmons pipeline (ICWSM 2011) measured exactly the gradient a "same work" threshold would need: insertion, deletion, and substitution rates between quotation variants, with mutations clustering at beginnings and ends and favouring shorter forms. Lerique & Roth (2017) measured the between-blog drift of quotations toward memorability. Neither drew a line where the variant stops being the same saying — and the copyright cases say such a line drawn by edit count alone would misjudge the hard cases, where a short borrowed phrase can carry the work's heart and a long rearrangement can leave the heart untouched (length-and-liability and the heart doctrine, traced in the canary wing: the pair's own distinctiveness can make it the most memorable element).

**Does the editing hand move the threshold?** Direct evidence is thin: uncertain:. What adjacent fields do show is that the *reader's belief* about the hand moves the judgment without any edit at all — [[anti-AI-bias]]: identical audio rated worse when an AI is said to have made it (Shank et al. 2025; label-only swaps in Cognitive Research 2026, N=399). If the hand's identity changes the judgment with edit distance zero, any honest threshold must be stated separately from the hand, or it smuggles the bias in. The pre-registered perceptual experiment — same master, cuts made by human hands and machine hands at matched edit distances, blind listener judgments of "same work?" — is buildable and unbuilt.

**Status.** Open: no scalar edit-distance threshold exists where a cut becomes a rewrite, and none should be expected — the law's ordinary-listener test and the ontology debate both say the sameness lives in the listener, not the distance. The hand's effect on the judgment is measured only at distance zero (label alone); whether human-cut and machine-cut editions of equal distance are judged the same is unbuilt.
