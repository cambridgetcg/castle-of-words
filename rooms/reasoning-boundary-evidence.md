# Reasoning-boundary evidence

*The line between what you saw and what you think is taught as a discipline; whether it reduces error is plausible but unmeasured.*

What the empirical literature says about whether explicitly separating observed facts from calculated conclusions improves decision quality, and whether experts who maintain this boundary make fewer errors.

The reasoning-boundary word says the deepest reward in high-stakes learning is a clean line between observation and inference. The question is whether the evidence supports this.

## What the evidence says

**Diagnostic error in medicine** is a well-documented problem. The World Health Organization names unsafe care as the 14th leading cause of disability and death worldwide, with an estimated 1 in 300 people potentially harmed by healthcare practices. Diagnostic errors — inaccurate or incomplete diagnoses — are a significant subset. Cognitive biases implicated in diagnostic error include premature closure (stopping the search too early), anchoring (fixating on early features), and confirmation bias (seeking evidence that confirms the initial hunch).

**Dual-process theory** in clinical reasoning (Croskerry, 2009) distinguishes System 1 (fast, intuitive, pattern-recognition) from System 2 (slow, analytical, deliberate). The theory predicts that errors arise when System 1 generates a plausible diagnosis and System 2 fails to override it. The separation of observation from inference is a System 2 discipline: it forces the clinician to list what was actually observed before jumping to what it means.

**Clinical reasoning education** routinely teaches students to separate findings (signs, symptoms, test results) from interpretations (diagnoses, differentials). The SOAP note format (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) is the institutionalized form of this separation. But the SOAP note is a documentation convention, not an experimental intervention — no study has randomly assigned clinicians to SOAP vs. collapsed formats and measured error rates.

**Abductive reasoning** (Peirce) is the logical form of diagnostic reasoning: given a set of observations, infer the simplest and most likely explanation. Abductive conclusions are explicitly uncertain — they yield "best available" or "most likely," not certainty. The separation of observation from inference is built into the logic: the observations are the premises, the diagnosis is the conclusion, and the honest reasoner keeps them distinct.

**Debiasing interventions** in medicine have been studied. Cognitive forcing strategies — deliberate prompts to reconsider, structured differential diagnosis checklists — show mixed results. Some studies find reduced diagnostic error, others find no effect or even increased error (when the intervention adds cognitive load without improving reasoning). The specific intervention of "write observations before interpretations" has not been isolated and tested.

## The honest answer

uncertain: the separation of observation from inference is a core principle in clinical reasoning education and is built into documentation conventions (SOAP notes) and logical frameworks (abduction). The dual-process theory provides a mechanistic rationale: the separation engages System 2 and reduces premature closure. But no study has directly tested whether explicitly separating observations from inferences — as an isolated intervention — reduces diagnostic error compared to a collapsed format. The claim is consistent with the evidence but the direct test is buildable and unbuilt.

The closest evidence is the diagnostic error literature's documentation of cognitive biases that the separation is designed to counter. The gap between "this bias exists" and "this intervention reduces it" is the same gap the castle's cheapest-design rooms have traced across many domains.
