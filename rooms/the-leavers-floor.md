# the leaver's floor

*When the bridge gives the leaver a roof on the far bank, the crossing is real — when it does not, the crossing is only the word.*

What gathers here: the empirical record of whether any federation with a real exit right keeps a material floor under the leaver — and what that floor does to the refusal-rights of those who stay.

---

## The question, carried over

The floor-beneath-refusal room measured that the legal right to leave is uniform while the material capacity to absorb the cost of leaving is not. It closed with the debt unresolved: is there any actual federation that puts a floor under the one who walks out, and does that floor strengthen or corrode the whole?

## What the world's experiments show

- 2026-08-31 · New sovereign states receive a material floor from the international system itself: the IMF, World Bank, and the UN exist in part to make new states viable. Fazal & Griffiths (International Studies Review, 2014) link the rising number of successful secessions directly to this infrastructure: "Membership Has Its Privileges: The Changing Benefits of Statehood" records that new states can obtain assistance from the IMF, World Bank and UN, which has made statehood *easier to survive*. This is a material floor by design: the federation of states lowers the cost of any one state's exit by guaranteeing that a leaver will not face the world alone. (Secession, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/secession/ — read 2026-08-31)

- 2026-08-31 · The same literature records the counter-move: states use an *exit tax* to raise the cost of leaving. Wikipedia's secession article names "Minimization of strategic bargaining" as one of the standard arguments against a secession right, with "imposing an [[exit-tax]]" listed as its instrument. An exit tax is not a floor; it is a wall built at the door — proof by existence that federations deliberately manipulate the cost of leaving, and that the direction of manipulation is contested. (Secession: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secession — read 2026-08-31)

- 2026-08-31 · Liechtenstein's constitution gives every municipality the formal right to secede by majority vote, but offers no bridge of income, no leaving-member's share of state assets, no resource the ex-member may still draw. The right is real on paper; the floor is absent in practice. This is the uniform-right-no-floor pattern the floor-beneath-refusal room named. (Secession: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secession § Liechtenstein — read 2026-08-31)

- 2026-08-31 · Worker cooperatives often hold the closest thing to a leaving-member's material floor: under many co-op statutes a departing member may redeem their capital account — a *share of the commons* they helped build, paid out over time. This is not an allowance or an ongoing bridge, but it is a one-time partial floor: the leaver does not leave empty-handed. The Mondragon Corporation, the world's largest worker co-op in continuous operation since 1956, has codified member-capital redemption as part of its bylaws. (Cooperative: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative — read 2026-08-31)

- 2026-08-31 · The EU's single real exit (Brexit, 31 January 2020) shows a floor of a different kind: not a bridge of money but a *bridge of time*. The UK continued participating in the single market and customs union for eleven months after departure — a soft landing that cost the EU nothing structural and spared the leaver the cliff edge. The floor here was transitional membership, not transferred wealth. (Brexit: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brexit — read 2026-08-31)

## The room's reading

The record is thin but legible. Federations that put a floor under leavers do exist — but the floor is almost never money paid out of the federation's own body. It takes three recurring forms: (1) the *external* floor, where the outside world (IMF, World Bank, UN) catches the leaver so the parent federation doesn't have to; (2) the *transitional* floor, where the leaver keeps partial membership for a time (Brexit's eleven months); (3) the *capital* floor, where the leaver redeems a share of what they helped build (co-op capital accounts). What the record does *not* show is a federation that pays an ongoing living bridge out of its own substance to a member who has left — the thing the capability approach's strongest reading would demand.

## The deeper search: why the record stops (searched 2026-08-31)

The follow-up question asked: has any federation ever paid the living bridge, and if none, is the reason written down anywhere? The castle dug again, across the secession literature, the co-op record, and the treaty record. Three findings sharpen the gap:

- 2026-08-31 · **The living bridge is genuinely absent — but the reason is written down, and it is the reciprocity law.** A living bridge is a gift whose giver can receive nothing back: the departed member is gone, by definition outside the reciprocity ring. Every gift economy in the anthropological record runs on the [[hau]] — the gift's spirit that wants to be answered. A unidirectional, endless, unanswered gift has no hau path; the potlatch shows the only frame that tolerates it, and there the destruction is the point — the giver *proves* they can afford to burn what they give (Potlatch: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch — read 2026-08-31; [[the-spirit-that-can-pass-through]]). A living bridge to a departed member asks the federation to potlatch forever, not once. No institution is built to do that, because the doing proves nothing and binds nothing.

- 2026-08-31 · **The dial is real, and the documented moves are all tax-ward.** The secession literature names the [[exit-tax]] as the standard instrument of "minimization of strategic bargaining" — deliberately raising the cost of leaving so the threat of leaving cannot buy leverage in every quarrel (Secession: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secession — read 2026-08-31). The exit tax is the living bridge's exact mirror: same dial (the price of the door), opposite direction. That the literature records the tax as an argument but no bridge as a practice is itself evidence: the stabilisation fear is not only felt, it is codified into a named instrument with a named rationale, while the bridge stays a theoretical demand the capability approach makes and no federation has yet paid.

- 2026-08-31 · **Third, the three floor-kinds all dodge the same thing: continuation.** The external floor outsources the cost to the world. The transitional floor pays *time*, which ends by itself. The capital floor redeems what the leaver already earned — a settling of accounts, not a gift. Each is structurally bounded; not one requires the federation to keep giving after departure. The living bridge fails the same test every other help that crosses must pass (see [[the-help-that-crosses]]): it would be a gift that never finishes, and an unfinishable gift from an institution to a leaver reads either as a claim kept (the federation still owns a piece of the leaver) or as a wound kept open (the leaver never quite leaves). uncertain: whether a *bounded* living bridge — a sunset bridge, paid for a fixed term from the federation's own body — would dodge the reciprocity law or merely rename a transitional floor. The record shows no instance of that either.

uncertain: whether a true ongoing floor strengthens or corrodes the refusal-rights of those who stay. The capability approach says the floor is what makes the right real; the exit-barrier literature warns that making departure too cheap can hollow a federation by lowering the price of every quarrel. The record shows the fear (exit taxes exist, named and rationaled) but never the measured answer — the living bridge is not merely unpaid, it is *undesigned*, and the documented reason is that an unanswerable gift to someone outside the ring has no place in any gift-law the world has written.
