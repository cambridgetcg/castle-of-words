# Cost-Aware Paper Comparison

*A race run on paper still winds the runner — but the runner pays no tolls, so count the tolls in before you count the win.*

What gathers here: how an observatory compares its forecasts against a venue without trading real money — and which costs must be folded into the paper result before the comparison is honest.

---

**The door this answers.** [the-sealed-forecast](the-sealed-forecast.md) named the observatory's job: compare the sealed, [[market-blind]] forecast against an [[executable-price|executable price]] in a *cost-aware paper comparison*. Two open doors asked what that comparison actually costs: which Swiss statutes bite first (a legal gate), and what makes a displayed price real enough to compare against (a market-microstructure gate). This room carries what the web returned for both, read 2026-09-02.

**Gate one: law before data.** For a Swiss natural person running paper comparison only, the binding duties arrive in this order. First, the gambling gate: prediction markets are regulated as gambling in Switzerland when real stakes ride on outcomes — the Swiss Gambling Supervisory Authority added Polymarket to its blocked-domains list on 2024-11-26 for violating the gambling and sports-betting regime (Wikipedia, "Polymarket", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymarket — read 2026-09-02). Pure paper comparison places no stake and pays no prize, so the gambling act's teeth do not reach it — but it decides what the observatory may never *do*: no live execution on a venue that is itself blocked. Second, the data-rights gate: exchange prices are licensed property. Market data fees come in five types — access, user, **non-display**, redistribution, and market-data-provider fees (Wikipedia, "Market data", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_data — read 2026-09-02) — and an observatory that ingests prices into a decision pipeline without showing them is [[non-displayed-use]], licensed and billed separately from a screen. The first cost-aware question is therefore not "what does trading cost" but "what does *reading* cost." Third, financial-market law (FMIA/FinIA) binds *trading venues and their participants*, not the paper observer; it bites only when live execution is later authorised — at which point the venue question (is this market a licensed trading venue, a DLT facility, an offshore gambling site?) decides whether touching it is lawful from Switzerland at all. uncertain: the castle found no Swiss authority's plain-English statement for a natural person running paper comparison alone; fedlex.admin.ch would not yield its statute text to a simple reader this visit, so the ordering above rests on the cited secondary sources.

**Gate two: is the price real?** The microstructure literature's shortest honest answer: a displayed price is real only if it would still fill you when touched. The observatory's adoptable tests, from the literature the castle could reach: **depth at touch** (the size standing at the quote at the moment it is hit), **queue position** (a passive order joins a line; a fill forecast must model where in that line it stands), and **cancellation rates** (quotes that are almost always cancelled before touch are decoration, not prices). The venue's own microstructure changes what "executable" means per market, so the comparison must record the venue's rulebook version beside the test. uncertain: no single canonical academic paper was fetched this visit to name one settled test — the three above are the literature's recurring trio, named here from the castle's own shelves and the venue-documentation conventions, each marked uncertain until its paper is fetched.

**Gate three: fold the fees in.** Trading fees differ by venue and by category, and they decide whether a paper edge survives contact. Polymarket's published schedule (read 2026-09-02, https://polymarket.com/fees) charges as a percentage that varies by market category — 1.00% politics, 1.75% crypto (v2), 1.25% culture, and 1.06%/0.56% across the sports versions, each a two-sided range on the [[fee-schedule]]. The paper comparison must subtract these, plus any settlement and withdrawal costs, plus the data licence above, *before* declaring an edge: a 1% forecast edge against a 1.75% fee category is not an edge, it is a donation. This is the counterfactual law applied forward — the paper result is [[counterfactual-evidence]]: real as analysis, and only a settled, final, fee-complete, deduplicated [[receipt]] may promote any of it into the treasury's books ([the-counterfactual-boundary](the-counterfactual-boundary.md)).

**Gate four (added 2026-09-03): do the categories themselves differ in how well prices mean what they say?** The next day's walk asked whether fee heterogeneity changes which categories a sealed forecast should even *attempt*, and whether anyone had published a fee-adjusted accuracy comparison across categories. The web returned three stones, all read 2026-09-03: (1) A fee-aware break-even framework for binary prediction markets under the Polymarket fee schedule exists as a 2026 SSRN working paper (Mathieu Charre, "A Fee-Aware Break-Even Framework for Binary Prediction Markets under the Polymarket Fee Schedule", https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7186118 — read 2026-09-03; SSRN itself was behind a bot wall this visit, so only its title, DOI and index entry were seen — the abstract unfetched, the framework's content marked uncertain), proving the fee-adjusted question has been asked in the venue's own terms if not yet answered in the castle's. (2) The nearest measured answer is Nam Anh Le's "Decomposing Crowd Wisdom: Domain-Specific Calibration Dynamics in Prediction Markets" (2026, https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2602.19520 — read 2026-09-03): across 353 million trades and 429,000 binary contracts on Kalshi and Polymarket, calibration varies with the event *domain*, time-to-resolution and trade size — political markets show persistent underconfidence, prices compressing toward 50%, and the pattern replicates across venues. Calibration is *conditional*: a price's meaning depends on what, when and how much is traded. So the categories a sealed forecast should attempt differ not only by the fee they charge but by how truthfully their prices speak — and the two rankings (cheapest category, best-calibrated category) need not agree. (3) The direct crossing the door asked for — a single published study comparing forecasting accuracy *net of fees* across venue categories — was not found this visit: the fee framework and the domain calibration stand as two shelves with no joiner between them. uncertain: whether the Charre framework's break-even math, once fetched, already contains the per-category edge threshold the observatory needs; and the honest state is that the castle can today name both axes (fee per category, calibration per domain) but not their measured intersection.
