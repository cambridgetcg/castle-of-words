# The Moving Line

*A medal that meant much at three winners in a hundred means nothing at a hundred winners in an issue.*

What gathers here: whether the earned-rarity line — the adoption level where a seal's signal fades — itself moves as a field's baseline sharing norm moves, answered from cross-field badge evidence gathered in one afternoon.

---

**The door.** The [earned-rarity-line](earned-rarity-line.md) room found the line is real but unmapped: a badge's power is relative to the field's baseline sharing norm — a badge at 25% adoption is meaningful where 5% share and meaningless where 80% share. The door asked the sharper form: as a field's norm rises (say, from 20% to 50% data sharing), does the badge's meaningful ceiling relocate upward with it (only the top fraction ever counts), or does the badge saturate into noise regardless of the norm (no badge means anything once it is common)? The mapping study has never been run — no one has introduced the same badge at different baselines and watched the effect — but the cross-field record has, by accident, already scattered the data points along the curve.

**The evidence, field by field.** Three journals now sit at three points on the adoption axis, and their badge fates trace the line's direction:

- **Psychological Science, 2014–2016: the line at low baseline.** Kidwell et al. introduced Open data and Open materials badges when data sharing was under 3%. The badge was a stark deviation from the norm, highly visible, and it carried meaning: sharing rose to 23% by 2015 and near 88% of articles reported availability statements by 2017. The badge moved a field *because* almost nobody had one (read 2026-08-30 — [Kidwell et al., "Badges to Acknowledge Open Practices: A Simple, Low-Cost, Effective Method for Increasing Transparency," PLOS Biology 2016, doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1002456](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1002456)).

- **Biostatistics, 2017–2018: the line at rising baseline.** Rowhani-Farid et al. repeated the design where the norm was already higher. The effect survived in relative terms — badged articles shared data 3.9× more often — but shrank in absolute ones: +7.6 percentage points. The badge still meant something, but its ceiling was lower and its pull weaker (read 2026-08-30 — [Rowhani-Farid et al., "Badges for sharing data and code at Biostatistics: an observational study," F1000Research 2018, doi:10.12688/f1000research.13477.2](https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.13477.2)).

- **Psychological Science, April 2019: the line at saturation.** The journal published its first issue in which *every* Research Article carried the Open Data badge — 100% adoption, the badge as wallpaper by design. Twelve researchers then tried to reproduce the issue's articles: all 14 provided at least some data, but **only one was exactly reproducible**, three more reproducible with minor deviations. The universal badge had stopped certifying anything beyond a nominal statement. The signal the badge was built to carry — this work backs its claims with usable material — had diluted into a participation mark (read 2026-08-30 — ["What's in a Badge? A Computational Reproducibility Investigation of the Open Data Badge Policy in One Issue of Psychological Science," Psychological Science 2023, doi:10.1177/09567976221140828](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976221140828)).

**And the control: a badge that never joined the norm.** Rowhani-Farid's 2020 randomized trial at *BMJ Open* offered badges by email to authors of already-accepted papers — no integration into the journal's pages, no community norm around the badge at all. Badges did not noticeably motivate sharing: odds ratio 0.9, two datasets shared per 80-article group. A badge that is neither rare-visible nor norm-integrated is not even on the curve — it carries no signal to dilute (read 2026-08-30 — [Rowhani-Farid et al., "Did awarding badges increase data sharing in BMJ Open? A randomized controlled trial," Royal Society Open Science 2020, doi:10.1098/rsos.191818](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.191818)).

**What the crossing answers.** The four points do not sit on one fitted curve — the studies differ in design, field, and decade, and the honest reading must hold them loosely. But the direction is consistent and it is *saturate, not relocate*: as adoption rose toward the norm, the badge did not keep its meaning by becoming the badge of an ever-higher elite; it became part of the furniture, and the furniture stopped saying anything. At 3% baseline the badge moved a field. At 100% adoption it certified nothing a reader could act on. The earned-rarity line is where the badge still deviates from the norm — and the line does not travel upward with the norm; the norm, once it absorbs the badge, erases the line. This is the canary wing's genericide law confirmed at the level of journals: the parade fades the fingerprint, and the parade the badge starts is the one that absorbs it.

**The design consequence, with its honest limit.** A badge that wants to keep meaning must refuse to become the norm it celebrates: the bar must stay where earning it remains a deviation — which means [the-game](the-game.md)'s castle law (rarity counted, never assigned) is not merely fair but functional, and a seal that succeeds *should* see its own power decay, by design of the world, not by any failure of its own. uncertain: the four points are not one experiment; a true dose-response study — the same badge introduced at 20%, 50%, and 80% baselines — remains buildable and unbuilt, and it might yet find a relocatable component (a badge of stringency that re-anchors at each norm). What today's cross-field record rules out is only the strong relocation claim, that a badge's meaning simply floats upward with its norm untouched.
