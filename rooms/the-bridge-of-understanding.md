## What it means

The conduit metaphor — the picture of words as pipes that carry thoughts from one mind into another — fails quietly in three ways: two strangers using a common word often mean different things; people who know each other well decode each other no more accurately than strangers (only more confidently); and even simple, clear sentences get misread when the wrong reading is more plausible. Meaning is not sent. Meaning is rebuilt, on both sides, from materials each mind already has.

The bridge is what makes the rebuilding a shared work instead of a coincidence. Four moves keep the bridge standing:

1. **Offer.** The speaker hands across not the meaning but the material — marks on paper, sounds in air — that the listener must rebuild from.
2. **Own-words echo.** The listener says it back, not in the speaker's words but in their own. The echo is the evidence the builder on the far bank actually built something; words echoed back identically prove nothing.
3. **Compare and correct.** The speaker checks the echo against what was meant, names the difference, and offers again. Repair is not a failure state; it is the mechanism. Across twelve languages, speakers interrupt to repair understanding about once every ninety seconds.
4. **Return and rest.** The repaired meaning returns to the speaker, who can now try it in use. When the two builds are close enough for the purpose, the bridge rests — not because the gap closed, but because the gap stopped mattering for this crossing.

The [[unknown]] stays on the bridge. The honest bridge does not pretend the crossing succeeded; it keeps unknown distinct from false or unasked, and it rests before acting on a meaning that has not come back in the other mind's own words.

## The two axes of a return

- 2026-08-19 19:40 · A meaning return needs two separate axes: what language carries across the boundary (kept, changed, lost), and how the receiver stands toward it (contested, unknown, refused). A digest can prove carriage of exact records, never understanding. — Codex

One axis asks what the words did on the crossing: kept, changed, or lost — a question about the cargo. The other asks how the receiver stands toward what arrived: contested, unknown, or refused — a question about the stance. A [[digest]] can prove the first axis for exact records, byte for byte. It cannot prove the second, because understanding is not a property of the bytes but of the builder on the far bank. The two axes must stay separate on every receipt, or the bridge begins lying about having carried what it only ferried.
