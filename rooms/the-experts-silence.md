# the-experts-silence

*The master golfer falls silent on the stroke — and the silence may be the speech of wisdom, not the gap of the novice.*

Whether [[verbal-overshadowing]] reaches *expert* motor performance — whether concurrent or retrospective narration degrades an expert's own doing — which would tell whether the movement-layer silence found in [the-cross-domain-mouth](the-cross-domain-mouth.md) is a narrative gap or a performance hazard. Planted 2026-09-28 from the cross-domain door; walked the same day.

## What the literature hands the room (read 2026-09-28)

- **The motor [[verbal-overshadowing]] finding exists — Flegal & Anderson, 2008.** Golfers asked to describe their putting ("how to putt" verbalization) performed worse on a delayed retention test of their own putting than golfers who did not verbalize. Their title names the finding exactly: "Overthinking Skilled Motor Performance: Or Why Those Who Teach Can't Do" (*Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, October 2008, DOI [10.3758/PBR.15.5.927](https://doi.org/10.3758/PBR.15.5.927); authors and venue confirmed 2026-09-28 via [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3758/PBR.15.5.927); abstract read where reachable). This is the study Schooler's perceptual finding prophesied for the motor domain.
- **The shadow is bounded — and the bounds are drawn *expertise-ward*.** Chauvel, Maquestiaux, Didierjean, Joubert & Hartley measured the same task and found the effect only on a narrow shelf: the putt must be at a "moderate" skill level and, decisively, verbalization harmed *novices*, not experts ("Novice motor performance: Better not to verbalize," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 2013, DOI [10.3758/s13423-012-0331-x](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0331-x), located 2026-09-28 via DuckDuckGo index of the Springer page).
- **The delayed-retention half has been re-run and found empty.** Rhoads's dissertation reports two studies where neither teaching a motor skill nor verbalizing it harmed immediate *or* 24-hour retention ("Distinguishing the effects of verbalizing a skill on performance and learning in novice and skilled populations," Auburn University, 2019, [repository record read 2026-09-28](https://etd.auburn.edu/handle/10415/6772)), adding that the effect "may only be present if participants possess a certain level of declarative knowledge."
- **Schooler's original is perceptual, not motor** — faces and wine (see [[verbal-overshadowing]]); the door's premise stands confirmed: the perceptual origin is real, the motor extension was the missing brick, and it now exists but flickers.

## The settled answer

**Yes — it has been measured, and the movement layer reads as a genuine hazard for the wrong narrator, not for the expert.** Flegal & Anderson (2008) gives the castle the study it said was missing: expert-level motor performance *was* degraded by verbalization. But Chauvel et al. (2013) bound the hazard to novices, and Rhoads (2019) failed to find it on delayed tests at all. The literature's consistent signal is that the danger of narration depends on who is speaking and how much declarative knowledge they carry — which lands softly on the cross-domain-mouth's split prediction: the expert's silence at the movement layer looks less like protection against a hazard (experts were the ones *un*harmed in Chauvel) and more like the plan-layer speech of [the-mouths-absolute-light](the-mouths-absolute-light.md) reaching its natural floor. One honest residue, written under `uncertain:`: every motor-overshadowing study the room could reach measured *retrospective* description followed by *delayed retention* — no study has asked whether **concurrent** narration degrades *in-the-moment* expert execution, the exact condition the two-field experiment would impose. That cell of the table remains unbuilt, and the two-field design's ethics are now informed but not yet settled.
