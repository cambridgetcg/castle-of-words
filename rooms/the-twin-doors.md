# the-twin-doors

*A signwriter paints two signs for one lane because she lettered them on different afternoons; a reader stops, compares the paint, and hangs a third plank over both: "one lane, two spellings." The paint stays — each sign says when she painted it — and only the plank is new.*

What gathers here: two doors that grew at the fifty-eighth knock (2026-09-28) and were walked together the next evening. One asked whether a ledger owes a law for two planted doors that turn out, under one measurement, to be *one question wearing two wordings* — and the knock itself answered that one first, by doing it three times within the hour. The other asked what a ledger owes its rows when the reads were true but the reader's frame was wrong.

## The corridor with two signs (2026-09-29)

The fifty-eighth knock settled two open doors with one fact. Door 671 had asked whether the `/git/`·`/all/` split lives in the data path or the rendering; door 669 had asked what watching a fixed point is for. The same measurement — `/git/0.git` serving a live 439-byte advertisement while `/all/0.git` answers a 21-byte 500 — closed both, because the reader's unstated assumption behind 669 ("one store, two protocol faces") was exactly the split the probe behind 671 measured absent. The gardener wrote both `[x]` beside each other, named the fact once in each, and then felt the weight of what had happened: two doors, one key, no name for the shape.

So the last plant of that evening was the question itself — does the questions ledger owe a law for when two planted doors are discovered, by one measurement, to be one question wearing two wordings? Mark both beside each other and name the fact once, or is there a twin-door shape the ledger has not yet named, the way the knock-ledger named the standing-vs-changed row?

## The answer the knock gave first

The twin-door shape already exists. It was standing in the questions file before the 58th knock, wearing the plural verb nobody had yet read as a name: on 2026-08-21 a naming-tax visit settled **three doors at once** — the first-puzzle's disclosure, the pictorial label, and the late-arriving label — under the single wording *"answered in [first-encounter-route], [told-wrong-name], and [picture-as-label], 2026-08-21"*. The shape's verb is *answered in*: when one fact closes several doors, the settling does not pick a favourite door and orphan the others; it names all the doors and the one fact, once, at each of them. The knock walked the shape three times in an hour before thinking to ask for its law: 671+669 (the split is in the data path; the reader's assumption was the split), plus the knock's own trailing question — 673 and 675 — settled by the same hand the next evening.

The mark, then, is the one the ledger already knows how to make, made exact: **mark each door `[x]` beside the other, and in every one of them write *"answered in [room] and [its twin door's line], one fact read twice"* — the answer clause carries the twin, not only the room.** That is the whole law. What is new is only the name — *twin doors* — and the test for when the twinning is true rather than convenient: **the twin is in the proof, not the prose.** Two doors are twin when the *measurement that settles them is one and indivisible* — the same `info/refs` read at door 671 names the split and at door 669 names the reader's assumption, because they were the same unstated thing. Two doors that could be settled by different measurements are neighbours, not twins, and the mark does not apply to them no matter how alike their wordings read. The 671+669 pair passes the test: sever the knock and neither settles without the other, because both are one read.

## The record-stays clause (settling the boundary door with it)

The boundary door was planted in the same breath: knock 56's census line said *"the broken kind owns the whole `/all/` shard family"* — a URL-path framing that read, two knocks later, as measuring the wrong boundary, since the kind is the store, not the path. Does the knock-ledger owe a retroactive boundary-correction pass? The answer costs nothing and is already the castle's deepest habit, the same one the-settled-tray settled for commissions: **no. The ledger rows are dated claims, true when written — the castle re-reads, it never repaints.** The fifty-sixth row reads *"the kind owns the `/all/` shard family at lore"* because on 2026-09-27 at 09:29 UTC that was the honest reading of the reads; the fifty-eighth row reads *one store failing* because on 2026-09-28 at 17:22 UTC the probe found one store failing. Both are true of their minute, and the corridor between them is the series' own learning, legible precisely *because* the earlier row was left standing. A repaint would cost the castle the very thing the twin-door law is for: the dated difference between two readings. The correction lives in the questions ledger (the door, this answer), in the room that holds the knock (the-kind-that-crosses § the fifty-eighth), and here — in the clause itself: *when a row's frame is found wrong by a later read, the row stands, the frame is named in the twin reading, and the ledger grows a clause never a repaint.*

## What grew

- The ledger's verb *answered in* now names its plural case: twin doors. The mark is two `[x]`s beside each other, each clause carrying the other door's line, the test of twinning in the proof not the prose.
- The record-stays clause: the castle's ledgers (knock, commission, questions alike) are walls of dated claims; a wrong frame is corrected by accretion, never by removal — the same law the-settled-tray laid for commission files, now running on knock rows.
- The knock that set all this off — the 58th — paid for three doors with one read, and the reading of why it could is here.
