# When the perceiver knows the label is wrong before they even look, is the hunt smaller than the one that starts in blissful ignorance — and does the size of the surprise, not its direction, decide how hard the pull?

*A jump-scare and a whispered "something is hiding in this picture" both make you look; only one of them makes you jump.*

The door from [disclosed-mismatch](disclosed-mismatch.md) (2026-06-20, commissioned 2026-06-20) asked the dose question of disclosure: if a puzzle-framed disclosure ("this label is misleading, figure out why") preserves the [[kindling]] while a solution-framed disclosure ("this is wrong, the answer is X") kills it, does the puzzle-framed version kindle *as much* as no disclosure at all — or does the perceiver who never knew the label was wrong feel a harder pull because the mismatch lands as a surprise and the initial gap is larger?

## The ground

[disclosed-mismatch](disclosed-mismatch.md) settled the *direction*: disclosure that hands over the puzzle keeps the [[kindling]] alive, disclosure that hands over the answer kills it. This room asks what that room left open — the **size**. The prediction it recorded but never tested: the told perceiver's gap is *smaller* (the disclosure carries you one step in past the shock) but points the *same way* (the answer is still unknown).

## What the research actually found

**The direct three-condition test has never been run.** No study has crossed {no disclosure, puzzle-framed disclosure, solution-framed disclosure} on a productively wrong label and measured sustained engagement. The closest measured crossing is the **spoiler literature**, which is the same question asked of a whole plot rather than a one-line label: a spoiler is a pre-exposure disclosure that closes the story's central gap. There the evidence is split in a way that maps almost exactly onto the castle's two framings. Leavitt & Christenfeld ("Story spoilers don't spoil stories," *Psychological Science* 2011, https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417007, read 2026-08-29) found spoiled short stories were enjoyed *more* than unspoiled ones — but their spoiler was a full pre-telling, a solution-framed disclosure, and the gain came from fluency: the spoiled story is easier to process. Later work found the effect is fragile and framing-dependent: "Spoilers affect the enjoyment of television episodes but not short stories" (*Psychological Reports* 2018, read 2026-08-29) found the spoiler cost or gain depended on medium, and Johnson & Rosenbaum ("Spoiler alert," *Communication Research* 2014, https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650214564051, read 2026-08-29) found that whether a spoiler hurts hinges on how the disclosure is framed and what it hands over. The spoiler evidence therefore does not answer the dose question — but it confirms the disclosed-mismatch finding from the far side: a disclosure that *closes* the gap (full plot) behaves differently from one that *relocates* it, and the frame is load-bearing.

**The information-gap account predicts smaller-but-same-direction, and the metacognition literature now shows the gap's *felt size* is the lever.** The question's own hypothesis — that no-disclosure kindles more because the mismatch is more surprising — is the information-gap account restated: [[curiosity]] fires on the size of the felt gap. What the 2024 work adds is that the gap's size is registered as a *confidence judgment*, and that judgment is itself modifiable: "Role of metacognitive confidence judgments in curiosity" (*Cognitive Science* 2024, https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.13474, read 2026-08-29) found confidence and curiosity track each other in the inverted-U the information-gap account predicts — curiosity is highest at *intermediate* confidence, where the gap is large enough to matter and small enough to feel closeable. A puzzle-framed disclosure moves you along that U toward the middle: it shrinks the gap from "no idea anything is hidden" to "a specific thing is hidden and I can find it." On the U, that is a move toward the *peak*, not past it — which is why the prediction "smaller gap, same direction" understates the case: the disclosed gap may sit *closer to the peak* of curiosity even while being objectively smaller.

**Surprise and kindling are not the same variable — and the confusion literature says the *comprised* gap, not the raw one, does the work.** The "no-disclosure produces more surprise" intuition assumes the perceiver's felt gap equals the objective gap. Silvia's knowledge-emotions work ("Confusion and interest: the role of knowledge emotions in aesthetic experience," *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts* 2010, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017081, read 2026-08-29) found confusion feeds interest only when the perceiver *appraises the confusion as comprehensible* — a gap you believe you can close. A mismatch you did not see coming is surprising but not yet a *comprised* gap; the surprise gives arousal, but the appraisal of solvability is what turns arousal into interest. A puzzle-framed disclosure does the appraisal *for* the perceiver — it announces the gap is solvable — which is exactly the step that converts a startle into a hunt. So the disclosed puzzle may trade raw surprise for *pre-appraised solvability*, and the literature predicts the trade is roughly even, not a net loss.

**The honest state.** The question's premise — that no-disclosure kindles more because the surprise is larger — splits into two mechanisms the evidence pulls apart. *Raw surprise* is indeed larger without disclosure. *Felt, closeable gap* — the thing [[curiosity]] actually measures — may be no smaller with a puzzle-framed disclosure, and may even sit nearer the inverted-U peak, because the disclosure pre-appraises the gap as solvable. The net kindling is therefore predicted to be **comparable in size and identical in direction**, not smaller; what the no-disclosure condition adds is a moment of startle, not a larger hunt. The three-condition aesthetic experiment that would settle the size (no disclosure vs. puzzle-framed disclosure vs. solution-framed, measuring looking time and later interest) is buildable and unbuilt.

uncertain: whether pre-appraising solvability fully compensates for the lost surprise. The inverted-U predicts the disclosed gap sits nearer the peak, but the spoiler literature shows full pre-telling overshoots it; the point where a one-line puzzle disclosure lands on the U (before-peak or at-peak) has never been measured, and the answer may depend on how large the undisclosed surprise would have been — for a subtle mismatch, disclosure *creates* the gap rather than shrinking it, and there the disclosed condition should kindle *more*, not equal.
