# The Maker Under the Seal

*A painter who knows the frame will be checked holds the brush a little differently.*

What gathers here: what a checkable provenance seal does to the one who makes the work, not the one who hears it — the audit effect turned inward. Read 2026-08-25.

---

**The question.** The seal rooms all face outward: they ask what a discoverable receipt does to the listener who finds it. But the seal is also a promise made before the work begins — and the maker knows it will be there. Does knowing the work will carry a checkable seal change how the human–AI pair works? Does any literature measure the maker's behavior, not the audience's?

**The answer, in one breath.** Three literatures agree, and none is about art. Social facilitation (Zajonc 1965) shows the mere presence of a watcher speeds the well-learned stroke and trips the novel one. Accountability research (Lerner & Tetlock 1999) shows that not knowing the checker's views makes the thinker more self-critical and complex — but knowing them makes the thinker simply shift toward them. Electronic performance monitoring (a 2022 meta-analysis of 70 samples) shows that being watched raises stress, lowers satisfaction, does not reliably raise performance, and slightly raises counterproductive work. The seal's imagined checker is not a neutral eye: it is a stage light, and the actor it shines on changes what they do.

**Social facilitation: the well-learned and the novel part ways.** Zajonc's drive theory (1965, *Science*, doi:10.1126/science.149.3681.269) holds that an observer's presence raises arousal, and arousal strengthens the dominant response. For simple or practiced tasks, that is facilitation; for complex or new tasks, it is interference. Markus (1978, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, doi:10.1016/0022-1031(78)90034-3) confirmed it unobtrusively: people dressed faster in familiar clothes and slower in unfamiliar ones when someone watched. Robert Cottrell's evaluation-apprehension refinement (1972) says the driver is not mere presence but the audience's power to judge; Henchy & Glass (1968) showed only an audience that can evaluate produces the dominant-response pattern. A provenance seal is precisely an audience that can evaluate — it names what it checks and whose hand checked it. The prediction for the maker: routine parts of the making (drafting, formatting, known workflows) go faster and surer under the seal; exploratory, uncertain, or risky parts (the novel image, the untried form) grow more cautious.

**Accountability: the maker's two postures.** Lerner & Tetlock's review (1999, *Psychological Bulletin*, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.2.255) sorts accountable decision makers by whether they know their audience's views. When the audience's views are unknown, the thinker preemptively self-criticizes: they consider more perspectives, use more complex reasoning, and are less overconfident — the audit effect as a broadening mirror. When the audience's views are known, the thinker simply shifts toward them — the audit effect as a narrowing target. A discoverable seal sits between these: the maker knows the checker will look, but does not know who will check or what they will prize. The honest prediction is preemptive self-criticism with an edge of strategic polish — more careful about the checkable parts, not necessarily more honest about the rest.

**Being watched at work: the meta-analytic reading.** The closest literature to a standing seal is electronic performance monitoring (EPM). A 2022 meta-analysis (70 independent samples, 233 effect sizes, *Computers in Human Behavior Reports*, doi:10.1016/j.chbr.2022.100227 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-25) found monitoring slightly decreased job satisfaction (r = −0.10), slightly increased stress (r = .11), showed no relationship with performance (r = −0.01), and a small positive relationship with counterproductive work behavior (r = 0.09). The seal is not surveillance — it checks the work's provenance, not the worker's keystrokes — but it shares the shape: a standing sign that someone will look. The maker under the seal is a worker under a lighter watch, and the weight of the watch is set by how much the maker feels it.

**The seal as a two-edged lamp.** The literature gives the seal a Jekyll-and-Hyde profile. For the listener, the seal is a door that stays shut until suspicion opens it. For the maker, the seal is already open — it is the condition of making. The same transparency that lets the listener rest can make the maker tense. The two effects are measured in different rooms of psychology: the audience effect measures the one who is watched while doing; the accountability effect measures the one who will be judged after doing. A provenance seal binds both into one object.

**The honest reading.** The maker-facing literature does not answer the question directly — no study has handed a human–AI pair a checkable provenance seal and measured how their work changes. But three adjacent shelves point the same way. The seal will speed the routine parts of making, slow the exploratory ones, and make the maker more self-critical about the parts the seal can see. Whether that care spreads to the parts the seal cannot see — whether the inward audit spreads from the provenance to the truth — is the untested seam.

**Buildable and unbuilt.** The direct test: two-arm study, same task, same tools; treatment arm knows the final work will carry a checkable provenance seal, control arm does not. Measure time spent on routine vs. exploratory moves, number of novel forms attempted, self-reported stress, and quality of the checkable and uncheckable parts scored separately by blind judges. The social-facilitation literature predicts faster routine performance and fewer novel attempts; the accountability literature predicts more self-critical revision; the EPM literature predicts a small stress cost with no net performance gain. None has been run.

uncertain: whether the seal's checkable scope (provenance, not quality) spares the maker the full weight of evaluation apprehension; whether repeated making under seals habituates the effect into background noise; whether the human in the pair carries the stress while the model carries the compliance — the EPM literature measures workers, not collaborators.
