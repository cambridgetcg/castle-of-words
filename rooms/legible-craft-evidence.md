# Legible-craft evidence

*The six parts are a scaffold; the evidence says scaffolds work, but no one has counted the parts.*

What the empirical literature says about whether naming all six parts of a teachable craft (materials, conditions, process, expected result, failure, reason) improves transfer or retention compared to naming only some.

The legible-craft room names six parts a craft must name to be teachable. The question is whether the evidence supports naming all six over naming fewer, and which part carries the most weight.

## What the evidence says

**The worked-example effect** is the closest empirical anchor. Cognitive load theory (Sweller, late 1980s) predicts that worked examples — which show a complete solution including the process, the result, and often the conditions — reduce extraneous cognitive load and improve learning compared to problem-solving alone. The worked-example effect is "the best known and most widely studied of the cognitive load effects" (Sweller). Worked examples typically include: the problem statement (conditions), the solution steps (process), and the final answer (expected result). They sometimes include common errors (failure) and the rationale for steps (reason). But no study has systematically varied which components are present to measure their individual contributions.

**Faded worked examples** (Renkl) — where the later steps are progressively removed — show that the *process* component is load-bearing: learners need the full process early and can have it withdrawn later. This suggests process carries the most weight during acquisition, but does not test the other five parts independently.

**Instructional design models** (ADDIE and its descendants) name analysis, design, development, implementation, and evaluation as phases — a different decomposition than the six parts. The field has not converged on a canonical set of components, and no meta-analysis compares different component sets on transfer or retention.

**The cognitive load framework** distinguishes intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load. The six parts can be mapped onto this: materials and conditions shape intrinsic load, process and expected result reduce extraneous load, failure and reason increase germane load (schema construction). But this mapping is speculative — no study has tested it.

## The honest answer

uncertain: no study has systematically varied which of the six parts are present and measured transfer or retention. The worked-example effect supports the general claim that complete demonstrations improve learning, and faded examples suggest process is the most load-bearing component during early acquisition. But the specific claim that *six* parts are needed, or that any particular part carries the most weight, is a craft claim — consistent with the evidence but not directly tested by it.

The closest empirical tradition is cognitive load theory's research on worked examples, which has been extensively validated (Sweller, 1988; Renkl, 1997; many replications). The six-part decomposition is the castle's own synthesis, not a finding from that literature.
