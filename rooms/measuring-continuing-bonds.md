# Measuring Continuing Bonds Types

*The bond is not one thing — but the scale that measures it is still learning to count.*

The continuing bonds theory was built from qualitative methods (Klass's ethnography of bereaved parents), and critics have noted the lack of quantitative evidence linking specific types of bonds to grief outcomes. The quantitative literature has since developed, but the question of whether it treats continuing bonds as a single construct or measures distinct types has a mixed answer.

## The Continuing Bonds Scale

The Continuing Bonds Scale (CBS; Field & Filanosky, 2010) is the primary quantitative instrument. It was developed to measure the degree to which a bereaved person maintains a connection with the deceased. The scale has been validated in multiple populations:

- **German validation** (Schindler et al., 2022): 364 bereaved participants. Confirmatory factor analysis supported a two-factor structure: externalized CB (illusions, hallucinations) and internalized CB (using the deceased as a secure base). Externalized CB was associated with complicated grief risk factors; internalized CB was associated with better adjustment.

- **Costa Rican men** (2025): Construct validity and reliability confirmed in a male bereaved sample, addressing the gender gap in grief research.

- **Multifaceted psychometric evaluation** (2026): A recent evaluation in 165 individuals bereaved of a romantic partner tested the proposed factor structure with confirmatory factor analysis.

- **Three-wave longitudinal study** (2025): Bereaved college students tracked over 3-month intervals. Continuing bonds mediated the relationship between unfinished business and grief outcomes.

## Does the literature treat continuing bonds as a single construct?

The answer is: increasingly no, but the shift is recent. The original 1996 anthology treated continuing bonds as a broad phenomenon — the fact of ongoing connection — without cleanly distinguishing adaptive from maladaptive forms. The CBS's two-factor structure (internalized vs. externalized) is now the dominant framework, and it does distinguish types.

But the distinction is still relatively coarse. The internalized/externalized split captures one dimension (is the bond held as an inner resource or an outer presence?) but may not capture others: secure-base vs. anxious attachment to the deceased, voluntary vs. intrusive bonds, bonds that provide comfort vs. bonds that maintain distress.

The quantitative literature has moved from "do continuing bonds exist?" (yes) to "which continuing bonds help and which hurt?" — but the typology is still developing. The CBS is the best available instrument, and its two-factor structure is empirically supported, but the field has not yet produced a fine-grained taxonomy of bond types with distinct outcome predictions.

## What the critics were right about

The critics who said continuing bonds theory lacked quantitative evidence were right at the time (1996). The theory was built on qualitative observation and clinical experience. The quantitative evidence now exists — the CBS has been validated, longitudinal studies have been run — but the evidence base is still thinner than for other grief constructs (e.g., complicated grief, now prolonged grief disorder in the DSM-5-TR).

The specific question — whether the *type* of continuing bond (internalized, externalized, secure-base, anxious) predicts grief outcomes — has been partially answered: externalized bonds predict worse outcomes, internalized bonds predict better ones. But the finer distinctions (secure-base vs. anxious internalized bonds) have not been cleanly measured.
