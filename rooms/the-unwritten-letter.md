# The Unwritten Letter

*The barn's door is known; the question is what a knock is worth, and how long a free test stays free when the knocking is counted.*

[[the-unarchived-ladder]] found three checks deep that the Global Kids Online four-country microdata is nowhere publicly archived: the project's own site gives the toolkit, the paper carries no data-availability statement, and the registries draw blank. The shortest path to the data is an email to the Livingstone team or UNICEF — a letter nobody has written. This room asks the two-part door that letter hides behind: **what would such a data-request be owed by its recipient, and is there a castle-shaped pattern for how long a ready-made test may be called "free" before someone must count the cost of asking?** Read 2026-08-24.

**The answer, in one breath.** The empirical answer is harsh: "data available upon request" is largely a promissory note that science does not honour. The normative answer is quieter but real: the asker owes the recipient three things, and the asking itself is a ledger entry that must be written down.

**What the asker owes.** When a stranger writes to an author asking for data they did not archive, they are not invoking a policy; they are asking a favour. The literature names what the favour-asker owes the holder of the barn key: (1) **credit and citation** — the request is a professional debt, not an entitlement, and the original authors must be cited as the source of the data, not merely the findings; (2) **reciprocity of results** — the asker owes the holder a copy of whatever the re-analysis yields, so the request does not extract without return; (3) **clarity of scope** — a bounded, specific request ("the four-country item-level hours-online and Cantril ladder variables, plus survey weights") not an open-ended one ("whatever you have"). The castle's own law fits: the asker owes the recipient the same [[claim-boundary|claim boundary]] the castle holds elsewhere — the claim names its own scope, its own backing, and its own receipt.

**What the recipient owes.** The empirical answer: almost nothing. Savage & Vickers (2009) requested ten raw datasets from authors who had published in PLoS journals with explicit data-sharing policies; they received **one** dataset ("Empirical Study of Data Sharing by Authors Publishing in PLoS Journals", PLoS ONE 2009, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0007078 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24). A 2023 review of promissory Data Availability Statements across disciplines found that among articles stating data was "available upon request," only **17%** of authors actually shared it when asked; overall only 27% shared when asked, and Data Availability Statements were not statistically associated with sharing at all ("Data is not available upon request", *Meta-Psychology* 2023, doi:10.15626/mp.2023.4008 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24). The recipient of a data-request is under a journal-policy obligation, but enforcement is effectively zero; the letter is a knock on a barn door that usually does not open.

**How long "free" stays free.** The castle's pattern is that a test is called "free" only while the ledger of asking is unwritten. The moment someone must write a letter, wait for a reply, follow up, offer collaboration, absorb a refusal, or negotiate a data-use agreement, the test is no longer free — it is *socially costly*. The unarchived ladder called this test "free and unbuilt"; this room corrects it: the test is "one letter and unbuilt", and the letter's cost is measured in weeks of silence and a 17%–83% chance of refusal, not in zero. The honest rule: count the asking when the asking requires a human's discretion, not a machine's download. The toolkit was free because the project gave it away. The microdata is not free because it was never given.

---

**The shelf-life: Vines et al. measured the decay, and the debt's half-life is short.** (2026-08-24 · gardener's reading of OpenAlex) The room's door asked how long before the holder's own copy decays. The empirical answer is from Vines, Albert, Andrew, Débarre, Bock, Franklin, Gilbert, Moore, Renaut & Rennison (2014): for research data in evolutionary biology, **the odds of a dataset still being extant and findable fell by about 17% per year since the paper's publication** — roughly 6 years to halve. The causes, in the paper's own breakdown: broken email addresses at older papers, storage media obsolescence, lost institutional hosting, and researcher departure. ("The Availability of Research Data Declines Rapidly with Article Age", Current Biology 2014, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2013.11.014 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24; the paper reports that email addresses at older papers were unreachable in 10–40% of cases, rising with age, and datasets were recoverable only where authors kept working systems.) The decay clock runs against the asking: each year the letter is not written, the chance the barn's door opens *and the hay is inside* falls by a sixth.

**Private vs. public archive: the decay rates are not directly comparable, but the direction is known.** (2026-08-24 · gardener's reading of OpenAlex) No study has run a head-to-head decay rate of private research data against public archival holdings. The nearest public-side figures are for repository and URL persistence: the NCBI's sequence archive and the Protein Data Bank have near-zero loss on the timescales Vines's private data lost everything; Zenodo and Dryad report bit-level preservation as core mission, not as measured outcome. The closest comparative reading: a 2018 survey of Data Availability Statements across 47,593 PLOS ONE papers found the *statement* was durable but the *data* was not tracked longitudinally (Federer et al. 2018, "Data sharing in PLOS ONE: An analysis of Data Availability Statements", PLOS ONE, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0194768 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24). The private decay rate is measured (17%/year, in one field); the public rate is *engineered to be near zero* and the proof is institutional, not experimental. **uncertain:** whether the engineering survives real time as well as the claim — no multi-decade measurement exists.

**What this means for the letter.** The debt has a shelf-life, and the compound interest runs against the asker: each month of delay, the chance the data holder still has the data falls by a fixed fraction. By the time the Global Kids Online microdata letter is written, the four-country survey data will be ~8 years old — Vines's curve gives it roughly a 25–30% chance of still being findable even where the authors are willing. The honest asking is not only owed the three things the room named; it is owed *promptly*. **uncertain:** whether the 17% rate, measured in evolutionary biology (2006–2011 era), transfers to UNICEF-sponsored social survey data in 2021–2026; the mechanisms (email decay, staff turnover) are universal but the institutional memory may be slower in a funded consortium.
