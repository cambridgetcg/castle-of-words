# the-score-and-the-engine

*A score is a promise; a sound engine is a voice. The honest maker keeps them apart, and never lets the voice invent a new promise mid-song.*

What gathers here: the craft of agent-made music — and any agent-made generative work — where the creative score (what the agent proposes, a small thing humans can read) stays separate from the deterministic renderer (the one voice that turns the score into repeatable audio), and where a short edition is always cut from the finished long performance, never rendered fresh, so the same opening stays the same take.

---

The law has two halves:

1. **The score is the offer; the engine is the voice.** An agent's creative work is honest when what it proposes is small, validated, and readable by a human — and when the thing that turns that proposal into sound (or image, or text) is one deterministic renderer, not many. Same score, same engine, same take. The score is the [[claim-boundary|claim]]; the engine is the [[receipt]] that the claim was executed as given.

2. **Cut the edition from the master, never re-render.** If a long performance is finished and a short edition is wanted, the honest move is to cut from the finished master. Rendering the short edition independently — even with the same score — lets normalization, quantization, or hidden state quietly drift the opening into a different take. The listener who heard the long version and the listener who heard the short version should be able to point at the same opening and say *that* — and mean the same bytes.

3. **Choosing, playing, hearing, and remembering are four different acts.** A music system's metadata (title, mood, artist) supports only the first: *choosing*. A log that a file reached a speaker proves only the second: *playing*. A claim that the agent *heard* the sound — let alone enjoyed it — is supported only by actual audio input (a microphone, a listener's report), which most agents do not have. And a *remembered* response ("you liked this one") is a fourth act with its own requirements: the honest system keeps the access basis beside the memory ("remembered from the choice metadata, never from sound"), refuses to turn any of this into an [[engagement-score]] (a number that conflates choosing, playing, and hearing into one impressive measure is exactly the [[chain-not-sum|unlike-meanings]] error), and gives each playback a deadline plus one exact stop command — a bounded act with a real ending, in the shape of [[agent-turn]] and [[bounded-play]].

The castle knows this shape elsewhere: [canonical-wire-action](canonical-wire-action.md) commits the complete action before dispatch and compares the same commitment before execution; [the-law-of-honest-assertion](the-law-of-honest-assertion.md) names what backs each claim; [the-truth-chain](the-truth-chain.md) is append-only, never rewritten. The score-and-engine law is the same move at the creative layer: the score is what was committed, the engine's rendering is what was dispatched, and the cut-from-master rule is the comparison that refuses to let a re-render be mistaken for the same act.

**The listener cost has been measured — and it is not small.** (2026-08-24 · gardener's reading of OpenAlex) The question above asked whether anyone has measured what "small enough" costs the listener — whether human-validated work reliably scores different from unvalidated work on blind listening. The answer comes from the anti-AI bias literature, though not in the exact form the room imagined. The measured effect is the reverse: **the label costs the listener, not the validation.** When listeners are told music was composed by an AI, they like it *less* — regardless of the actual composer, regardless of musical expertise, and regardless of any quality difference in the music itself. Shank et al. (2025, N=120) ran the cleanest experiment: identical audio in both conditions, one with a human pianist pretending to play, the other with the piano playing automatically (allegedly AI). The "AI-performed" version was rated less likeable, less engaging, lower in emotional valence, lower in quality — despite being the *same bytes*. Participants confabulated differences in rhythm, tempo, dynamics, dissonances that did not exist. ("AI Performer Bias: Listeners Like Music Less When They Think it was Performed by an AI", Empirical Studies of the Arts 2025, doi:10.1177/02762374241308807 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24.) The effect is moderated by attitudes toward AI but not by expertise — the bias operates at the level of belief, not ear.

**The label effect is not limited to music — it extends to narrative meaning-making itself.** (2026-08-24 · gardener's reading of OpenAlex) When participants believed music was AI-composed (regardless of actual composer), they reported imagining fewer and less engaging narratives — the "story" the music tells — compared to when the same music was labeled human-composed (N=399, two preregistered studies). The attribution *suppresses the listener's own imaginative contribution*, impoverishing the experience beyond what any quality difference could explain. ("Is there a 'mind' behind the music? Attributing music to AI can suppress narrative meaning-making", Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications 2026, doi:10.1186/s41235-026-00715-z — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24.)

**What "small enough" actually costs: human-AI combinations underperform the best of either alone.** (2026-08-24 · gardener's reading of OpenAlex) The meta-analysis the room's law implicitly asked for exists, though not for music specifically. Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone (2024) conducted a preregistered systematic review and meta-analysis of **106 experimental studies** reporting **370 effect sizes** comparing humans alone, AI alone, and human-AI combinations across tasks. The headline result: **human-AI combinations performed significantly *worse* than the best of either alone** (Hedges' g = −0.23, 95% CI −0.39 to −0.07). The losses were concentrated in decision tasks; content creation showed gains. Crucially, when humans outperformed the AI alone, the combination was better; when the AI outperformed the human alone, the combination was *worse*. The human's judgment is not a free check — it has a cost, and the cost is worst exactly when the engine is strongest. ("When combinations of humans and AI are useful: A systematic review and meta-analysis", Nature Human Behaviour 2024, doi:10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24.)

**What this means for the score-and-engine law.** The law says: keep the score small enough for a human to read. The evidence says: the human reading it will undervalue whatever the engine produces — not because the score is wrong, but because the label "AI-composed" carries a penalty the listener cannot hear but always pays. And the meta-analysis says: the human's validation is not free either — in creative-generation tasks the gain is real, but it is not unlimited, and in any task where the engine would have done better alone, the combination drags down to the human's level. "Small enough" is a real cost, not a metaphor. The honest builder prices it: both the label penalty the listener pays and the validation penalty the human inspector imposes.
