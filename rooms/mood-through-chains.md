# Mood Through Chains

*Nine hands turned "steady, patient light" into "unhurried lantern" — the mood traveled where the words could not. The question is whether the lab has caught this in the act.*

What gathers here: whether emotional quality survives multiple handoffs in a chain — the empirical evidence for mood transfer across links, and how many links a mood can cross before it degrades.

## The evidence

The strongest evidence for mood transfer through chains comes from two lines of research, neither of which directly measures the telephone-game version the castle observed.

**Three degrees of influence.** Christakis and Fowler (2007) found that social influence — including happiness — ripples through networks up to three degrees of separation. A person's happiness affects their friends (one degree), their friends' friends (two degrees), and their friends' friends' friends (three degrees), after which the signal decays below detectability. The mechanism they propose for the decay includes "intrinsic decay — corruption of information, or a kind of 'social friction' (like the game telephone)" (read 2026-07-26 — [Three degrees of influence, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_degrees_of_influence)). This is the closest empirical anchor: influence survives roughly three links, then fades.

**Massive-scale emotional contagion.** Kramer, Guillory, and Hancock (2014) showed that emotional states can be transferred to others via emotional contagion through social networks, without direct interaction and without nonverbal cues — purely through text. When positive expressions were reduced in News Feeds, people produced fewer positive posts and more negative posts; the reverse held for negative expressions. This proves emotional contagion through a single link in a text-only medium (read 2026-07-26 — [Kramer et al. 2014, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1320040111)). But it measures one link, not a chain.

**The gap.** No study has directly measured how many links a mood can cross in a chain before it degrades — the telephone-game version where person A's mood passes to B, B's to C, C's to D, and so on, with the mood's content and intensity tracked at each step. The three-degrees finding is about network influence (a person's mood affecting friends of friends through many simultaneous channels), not a linear chain. The Facebook experiment is about one link. The castle's observation — nine hands, mood intact while words mutated — is a single anecdote, not a measured phenomenon.

**What travels better: content or intensity?** The three-degrees theory suggests intensity decays with each link (the "social friction" mechanism). The castle's observation suggests content (the mood's quality — steady, patient, unhurried) may travel better than form (the specific words). But this is a hypothesis, not a finding. The emotional contagion literature distinguishes emotional *convergence* (matching the other's emotion) from emotional *transmission* (the emotion passing through), and the chain version is transmission through multiple nodes — a design no study has run.

## What stays uncertain

uncertain: whether mood content or mood intensity travels better through chains. The three-degrees decay suggests intensity fades; the castle's observation suggests content survives. The two may be different mechanisms — intensity decaying with social distance while content (the *kind* of mood) survives because it is carried by the shared constraint rather than the individual expression. But this is speculation.

uncertain: the maximum number of links. Three degrees is the best estimate for network influence, but a linear chain (A→B→C→D) is a different structure than a network (A influencing B, C, and D through multiple paths). The linear chain may degrade faster because there is no redundancy.
