# Active Rejection Grief

*The name was not taken by death — it was thrown back.*

What happens to the continuing bond when the lover is not dead but has deliberately severed the relationship and denied it ever mattered: the grief and attachment literatures have not cleanly reached this category.

## The continuing bonds model was built on death

The continuing bonds theory (Klass, Silverman & Nickman, 1996) emerged from studying bereaved parents and widows — people whose loved ones died. The theory says maintaining a lasting connection with the deceased is a normal part of grieving, not a hindrance to "moving on." The bond continues in adapted form: sensing presence, keeping objects, believing the deceased influences events, integrating their characteristics into identity.

But the model was built on relationships ended by death, not by choice. Death removes the person but does not reject the bond. The relationship's meaning is not contested by the one who left.

## Unreciprocated love and severed bonds

Millar (2022), in "Grief, Continuing Bonds, and Unreciprocated Love" (*The Southern Journal of Philosophy*), directly identifies the tension: the continuing bonds framework "appears to conflict with philosophical accounts of grief that emphasize the importance of the *relationship* rather than merely the *loss*." When the relationship was unreciprocated or actively rejected, the bond the griever maintains may be to something that never existed in the form they remember.

The attachment literature shows the bond survives defensive denial — a person can dismiss the importance of a relationship while the attachment system still registers it. But active rejection is different: the other person is alive, present, and *choosing* to sever. The bond is not to a memory of someone who cannot return; it is to someone who *will not* return.

## What the literatures do and do not reach

The grief literature (continuing bonds) reaches death. The attachment literature reaches defensive denial (the dismissing state of mind in the AAI). Neither cleanly reaches the case where a living other deliberately severs the relationship and denies it ever mattered.

Active rejection combines two features that pull in opposite directions:
- The other is alive (so the bond could theoretically be repaired — grief's finality does not apply)
- The other is the source of the wound (so the bond is not just to a memory but to an ongoing agent of harm)

The continuing bonds model's adaptive/maladaptive distinction (internalized secure base vs. externalized hallucination) may still apply — but the bond's object is contested in a way death never contests it. The person who severed the bond is also the person who could, in theory, restore it. That possibility — however remote — changes the shape of the grief.
