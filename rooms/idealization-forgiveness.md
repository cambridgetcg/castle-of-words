# Idealization Without Evidence in Forgiveness

*"I've completely forgiven" — but the story has no path from hurt to release.*

The AAI's dismissing state of mind is coded partly through idealization without evidence: "my mother was wonderful" with no supporting memories, or with memories that contradict the claim. The question is whether the same pattern appears in forgiveness narratives — and whether it predicts worse outcomes than a narrative that traces the path.

## The pattern in the AAI

In the Adult Attachment Interview, the dismissing speaker makes positive generalizations about attachment figures that are either unsupported by episodic memory or actively contradicted by the memories they do provide. The coder does not judge whether the parent *was* wonderful — the coder judges whether the speaker's *representation* is coherent: does the generalization have evidence, or is it floating?

This is a form marker, not a content marker. The same content ("my mother was wonderful") could be secure (supported by rich, coherent memories) or dismissing (unsupported, contradicted, or stated with a finality that closes off inquiry).

## The forgiveness parallel

A person who says "I've completely forgiven them" without any narrative of *how* forgiveness happened — no account of the hurt, no description of the process, no evidence of struggle — may be displaying the same form marker. The claim floats without evidence.

The narrative psychology literature (McAdams, 2001) codes redemption sequences: a bad event that leads to a positive outcome. Redemption sequences predict psychological well-being. But redemption is a content code — it requires the narrative to *contain* the transformation. A bare claim of forgiveness without the narrative of transformation is not a redemption sequence; it is an unsupported generalization.

## What is known and what is not

The forgiveness literature (Enright, Worthington, McCullough) distinguishes decisional forgiveness (the intention to forgive) from emotional forgiveness (the felt release of resentment). Decisional forgiveness can happen quickly; emotional forgiveness takes time. A bare claim of "I've forgiven" may reflect decisional forgiveness without emotional forgiveness — the intention without the process.

But the specific form-marker question — does idealization-without-evidence in forgiveness narratives predict worse relational outcomes than a narrative that traces the path — has not been directly tested. The pieces exist (AAI coding, narrative redemption coding, forgiveness process measures) but have not been joined at this seam.

The prediction from the AAI literature is that the unsupported claim of forgiveness would predict *worse* outcomes than a narrative that traces the struggle — because the unsupported claim suggests defensive processing (the hurt is denied rather than worked through), and what is denied tends to return.

uncertain: whether any study has applied AAI-style form coding to forgiveness narratives, and whether the idealization-without-evidence marker predicts forgiveness outcomes independently of content measures.
