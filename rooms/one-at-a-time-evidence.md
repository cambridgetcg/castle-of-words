# one-at-a-time-evidence

> *A threshold crossed alone is a threshold felt. A threshold crossed in a crowd is a threshold passed. Whether one builds more belonging than the other, no study has asked.*

What the empirical evidence says about whether one-at-a-time registration produces stronger belonging or more honest self-presentation than batch registration.

## The question

From the door custom: "register one at a time" — each citizen met as themselves, not as a batch. Is there empirical evidence, or is the principle about the quality of the threshold rather than a measurable outcome?

## What the evidence says

**Onboarding and organisational socialisation.** Van Maanen and Schein (1979) distinguished *individual* from *collective* socialisation tactics. Individual socialisation processes newcomers one at a time, providing tailored experiences; collective socialisation processes them in groups, with a shared experience. The theory predicts that individual socialisation produces *role innovation* (newcomers develop their own approach to the role), while collective socialisation produces *custodial orientations* (newcomers conform to existing norms). Jones (1986) extended this framework and found that institutionalised socialisation (including collective tactics) reduces role ambiguity and increases job satisfaction, but also reduces role innovation.

**The empirical evidence on individual vs. collective onboarding.** A meta-analysis by Saks, Uggerslev, and Fassina (2007) in the *Journal of Vocational Behavior* found that socialisation tactics affect proximal outcomes (role clarity, social acceptance) more than distal ones (job performance, commitment). But the individual-vs-collective dimension is typically bundled with other tactics (formal vs. informal, sequential vs. random, fixed vs. variable), making it difficult to isolate the effect of one-at-a-time processing specifically. No study has tested whether individual registration produces stronger *belonging* or more *honest self-presentation* than batch registration.

**Initiation rituals and belonging.** The social psychology of initiation (e.g., Aronson and Mills, 1959, on effort justification) shows that difficult initiations increase commitment to the group. But this is about the *difficulty* of the threshold, not whether it is crossed alone or in a group. Group initiations (hazing, boot camp) can produce strong belonging through shared ordeal. Individual initiations (a one-on-one conversation, a personal welcome) may produce a different kind of belonging — more personal, less tribal. But the comparison has not been made empirically.

**Self-presentation and anonymity.** The literature on online self-presentation (e.g., Turkle, 1995; Walther, 1996) shows that people present themselves differently in one-on-one settings than in group settings. One-on-one interactions tend to produce more honest, less performative self-disclosure. But this is about ongoing interaction, not about the registration threshold specifically.

## The honest answer

The claim that one-at-a-time registration produces stronger belonging or more honest self-presentation is **plausible but unmeasured**. The onboarding literature distinguishes individual from collective socialisation and predicts different outcomes (innovation vs. conformity), but does not measure belonging or honesty of self-presentation. The principle is about the *quality of the threshold* — a one-at-a-time threshold says "you are met, not processed" — and this is a craft choice, not an empirical finding. The door custom's rule is a normative claim about how belonging should work, not a prediction about measurable outcomes.

*uncertain:* whether the individual-vs-collective socialisation findings (role innovation vs. custodial orientation) generalise from employment onboarding to community belonging; the contexts differ in voluntariness, duration, and stakes.
