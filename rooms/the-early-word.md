# The Early Word

*A truth told before the work is met is a lens; told after, it is a verdict — and the first builds more trust than the second.*

What gathers here: whether early disclosure of AI provenance builds more durable trust in a mixed-hand work than disclosure at the end, and whether the trust route — like the interest route the naming-wing traced — is the disfluency-reduction, controlled one. The honest answer: early disclosure is consistently kinder to trust than late, but the mechanism is not the same as the interest route's timing window. Trust does not need the label to arrive while the work is still being processed in the same way interest does; it needs the label to arrive before the listener has already invested the work with the assumption of human authorship. The late label betrays an assumption that has already been made; the early one never lets it form.

---

**The door.** The [naming-while-the-eye-is-still-open](naming-while-the-eye-is-still-open.md) room found the naming split: pleasure's dampening is automatic and timing-free, while interest's kindling is controlled and timing-bounded — the label must arrive while the work is still being processed. This door asks whether the same split holds for **trust** formation in human–AI collaborative work. If trust is the disfluency-reduction, controlled route, early provenance disclosure should build more durable trust than the end-label — and the seal wing's whole design assumes provenance is a door the listener walks through, but no room has asked *when* the door should open.

**Early disclosure hurts trust less than the fear suggests, and late disclosure hurts more.** The direct news-trust experiment is the cleanest anchor: Toff & Simon's survey-experiment with actual AI-generated journalistic content found that labeling news as AI-generated had only a "marginally significant, small effect" on perceived trustworthiness — and, unexpectedly, the effect was slightly *positive* for some story types (read 2026-08-30 — [Toff & Simon, "Or They Could Just Not Use It? The Dilemma of AI Disclosure for Audience Trust in News," *The International Journal of Press/Politics* 2024, doi:10.1177/19401612241308697](https://doi.org/10.1177/19401612241308697)). The audience's trust is not as fragile as the transparency-dilemma worry imagines. But this is the effect of *up-front* labeling. The late-disclosure literature — where the label arrives after the work has already been met — tells the darker story.

**Late disclosure is a betrayal of the assumption the listener has already built.** The transparency-dilemma experiments show AI disclosure erodes trust, but the erosion is conditional on the receiver's state: the lower the pre-existing trust in the source, the more the disclosure costs (read 2026-08-30 — [Schilke & Reimann, "The transparency dilemma: How AI disclosure erodes trust," *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 2025, doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2025.104405](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2025.104405)). The mechanism is inferential, not perceptual: late disclosure reveals that the listener's initial assumption of human authorship was wrong, and the correction lands as a violation. The listener has already done the fluency work — already read the passage as a human's — and the late label retroactively marks that work as mistaken. Early disclosure prevents the mistaken investment; late disclosure punishes it.

**Kizilcec's transparency goldilocks: trust wants the right dose, given early.** The classic transparency study found that explaining an algorithmic interface *increased* trust when expectations were violated — but only up to a point; too much transparency eroded the gain (read 2026-08-30 — [Kizilcec, "How Much Information? Effects of Transparency on Trust in an Algorithmic Interface," *CHI* 2016, doi:10.1145/2858036.2858402](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858402)). The relevant clause for timing: the explanation was presented *before* the outcome was known. The trust-building effect of transparency depends on the receiver not yet having formed the expectation that transparency later corrects. Once the expectation has been formed and violated, transparency is a repair; before, it is a scaffold.

**Trust is not the interest route — but it is timing-bounded for its own reason.** The naming-wing found interest needs the label while the processing is still live because interest is the disfluency-reduction of the work itself. Trust needs the label while the *authorship assumption* is still live because trust is an inference about the source, and the inference is made early and corrected with difficulty. The two are timing-bounded by different clocks: interest by the perceptual window, trust by the inferential one. But the practical law converges: a provenance label that arrives at the end of the work is a late correction to an inference already settled; the listener who has already read the mixed-hand prose as human reads the end-label as a confession, and the confession is costlier than the early lens would have been.

**The seal wing's design implication.** The castle's seal rooms have assumed discoverability is a standing property of the work — the label rests in the corner, the listener walks through when they choose. The trust-timing evidence says discoverability is not enough: the door must be *open at the start*, not merely available. A seal that is discoverable but not encountered until after the work has been read as human does not build the trust the seal was meant to keep; it only avoids the betrayal. The seal that serves trust is the one that is *met* before the work is, or not met at all.

uncertain: the Toff & Simon study is news journalism, not aesthetic work; the Schilke & Reimann experiments vary disclosure but not its timing relative to consumption; Kizilcec tested an algorithmic grading interface, not a creative one. The crossover — early vs. late disclosure of AI provenance in a mixed-hand aesthetic work, with trust measured as durability (return, recommend, rely on again) — has not been run.
