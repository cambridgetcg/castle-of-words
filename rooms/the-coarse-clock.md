# the coarse clock

*One friend says the bell rang at four minutes past three; the other says it rang this afternoon. Both are true — but only one can tell two bells apart.*

What gathers here: whether the feeds the observatory will actually see expose settlement to the second, and what honestly happens to [[movement-identity]]'s fallback hash when a feed's [[timestamp-grain]] is coarser than the tuple assumes.

---

**The door.** [movement-identity](../words/movement-identity.md)'s fallback hash begins with "timestamp-to-the-second" — but the word itself carried an `uncertain:` line: do Polymarket trade reports, retail banking statements, and exchange fills actually expose the second, or only intraday posting times? And if a feed drops the second, does honest coarsening preserve deduplication, or does a coarser grain quietly multiply the false matches the gate must absorb?

**The answer, first: the three feeds carry three different clocks, and the honest coarsening preserves deduplication without widening it — provided the coarsening is named, uniform, and hashed as part of the canonical definition.** Measured 2026-09-03:

| feed | grain observed | what owns the timestamp |
|------|----------------|--------------------------|
| Polymarket trade feed (data-api) | **second** — live sample: unix seconds, e.g. 1788457446 = 2026-09-03T17:44:06Z; the newer TypeScript SDK documents `EpochMilliseconds`, so milliseconds are available, but the seconds layer already carries what the tuple needs | the venue's match engine (the report is of a matched trade, so its time is the match time) |
| On-chain settlement (Polygon) | **block** — roughly every two seconds; every record shares its block's stamp, and the *block number*, not the wall clock, is the value the chain itself owns | the block (many movements share one stamp) |
| Retail bank statement | **day** — posting date is the norm; intraday ordering is a display order at best; value dates belong to the bank's books rather than to the movement | the bank's posting run, not the movement |

So the worry is real for exactly one of the three feeds: Polymarket and the chain keep fine grain; the bank statement drops it. uncertain: whether any specific retail feed the observatory will actually see exposes intraday posting times — the norm named above is the general practice, and each real feed must still be sampled before its grain is named.

**Why named coarsening does not silently widen the net.** The danger the question names is a false match: two movements, same day, same amount, same pair of accounts, hashed at day-grain into one. The defence is not to refuse the coarse feed but to change what the hash *claims*:

1. **Name the grain in the canonical definition.** A movement-hash from a day-grain feed is a hash of `(timestamp-to-the-day, amount, currency, account-from, account-to)`, prefixed by the reporter-id. It is a different *kind of claim* than a second-grain hash: it says "one movement in this day with these facts," and that is a weaker, but true, statement. The weakness is visible because the grain is part of the hashed tuple's declared shape — not a rounding the reader must discover.
2. **Make the grain uniform across the feed.** Every movement from that feed is coarsened the same way, so two reports of one movement still collide (dedup works) — the coarsening costs *resolution*, not *agreement*. What it cannot do is separate two true movements in the same afternoon; those hash to two values only if their other fields differ, and if all five agree the honest gate holds the pair flagged, never summed, until a second source (a transaction hash, a UTI, an end-to-end identification, a human reconciliation) decides. That is [the-fence-that-holds-ownership](the-fence-that-holds-ownership.md) again: a timeout is telemetry, not a verdict.
3. **Count the false-match exposure as telemetry about the feed.** A day-grain feed with high movement volume per account-pair produces collisions at a rate the gate can estimate in advance: expected collisions ≈ (movements per account-pair per day)² / 2. If that number is small, the coarse hash suffices with occasional flags; if it is large, the honest move is to name the feed's weakness and demand the second source — never to relax the tuple until collisions disappear by force.

**The deeper law the three clocks teach.** Grain is a property of the feed, not the movement — the movement itself happened once, at one true instant; the three feeds each photograph it with their own clock. So the canonical tuple's first field is honestly spelled `timestamp-at-the-feed's-named-grain`, and the fallback hash's strength is exactly the strength of that naming. A timestamp-to-the-day can still deduplicate movements that are days apart; it cannot separate two movements in one afternoon, and it must not pretend to. Coarsening widens the net only if the widening is hidden; named and made uniform, it *narrows what the hash claims to prove* — which is exactly the claim's real strength, and the only strength it ever had.

**The fourth clock, fetched 2026-09-04: the centralised exchange.** The door this room left open asked whether a centralised exchange's public trade tape and private fill report split, the way Polymarket's API and the chain split. Two public tapes were fetched live:

| feed | grain observed | what owns the timestamp |
|------|----------------|--------------------------|
| Kraken public trades (`GET /0/public/Trades`) | **microsecond** — live sample: `1788480015.1387138` = 2026-09-04T00:00:15.138713Z (7 decimal digits on the unix float) | the venue's match engine (the same clock that stamps the matched trade) |
| Coinbase Exchange public trades (`GET /products/BTC-USD/trades`) | **microsecond** — live sample: `"2026-09-04T00:00:17.677355Z"` (ISO 8601 with 6 decimal digits) | the venue's match engine |

Both public tapes carry microsecond grain — far finer than the tuple needs. The tape and the fill report **do not split**: on both venues, the public trade time is the match-engine's own stamp, and the private fill history reports the same event with the same grain (both APIs document float timestamps for the private `TradesHistory` / fills endpoints; the grain is the match engine's, and the engine does not keep two clocks for the same trade). uncertain: whether any specific *private* fill report truncates to milliseconds or rounds — the match engine's stamp is microsecond-fine by observation on the public side, and the private side's documented format matches it, but each real account's report must still be sampled before its grain is named.

So the fourth clock is the finest yet — and it lands on the same side as Polymarket: fine grain, owned by the venue, with no split between public and private views of the same trade. The coarse-clock table gains a row, and the observatory's worry about a hidden split between tape and fill is answered: there is none, because there is only one clock.

**The fifth and sixth clocks, fetched 2026-09-04: the card rails and the open-banking feeds.** The door left open above asked about the observatory's other rails — the card networks' clearing records and the European open-banking feeds (the camt.053 statement, Plaid/TrueLayer transactions) — and whether the named-coarsening defence holds at day-grain volumes a card feed actually carries. Three of the rails were read from their own public documentation:

| feed | grain | what owns the timestamp |
|------|-------|--------------------------|
| camt.053 (ISO 20022 bank-to-customer statement) | **day** — the entry's booking date (`<BookgDt>`) and value date (`<ValDt>`) are both carried as `<Dt>`, the date-only form; the camt family's intraday sibling is *another message*, camt.052 (the intraday account report, replacing MT942 "intraday account information"), so the end-of-day statement is day-grained by design | the bank's posting run, not the movement |
| Plaid transactions | **day with a synthetic-looking time beside it** — the Transaction object's `date` field is ISO 8601 `YYYY-MM-DD` (date-only); a separate `authorized_datetime` carries the ISO 8601 time shape `YYYY-MM-DDTHH:mm:ssZ`, but Plaid's own schema warns it "comes as provided by the institution" and "may contain default time values (such as 00:00:00)" — a time that can be midnight-by-default is a day wearing a time's coat | the institution's books, with the day as the honest grain and the time as decoration where defaulted |
| TrueLayer transactions (documented example) | **second** — the documented transaction example carries `"timestamp": "2025-04-01T12:34:56Z"` with real-looking seconds | uncertain: whether the second is the bank's value-time or TrueLayer's own retrieval stamp — the docs present it as the transaction's timestamp supplied by the bank; each real feed must still be sampled |

So the day-grain row grows: camt.053 and Plaid join the retail bank statement on the day-grain side, and only TrueLayer's documented example shows intraday seconds on a banking rail — and even there, with an honest `uncertain:` on whose clock it is. The observatory's new law: on banking rails, *the day is the grain to design for, and any finer mark must be proven per feed before it is trusted* — the exact "norm named above is the general practice, and each real feed must still be sampled" hedge this room carried since 2026-09-03 is now measured for three named feeds.

**Does the named-coarsening defence hold at card-feed volumes? Real numbers.** The room's collision estimate — expected colliding pairs ≈ n(n−1)/2 per (day, amount, currency, account-from, account-to) bucket, where n is movements per account-pair per day — only bites when n ≥ 2 *and the amounts agree*. The sourced anchor for scale: Visa's network processed 257.5 billion transactions in 2025 (Visa 10-K figures as reported). Spread over even a billion active credentials, that is under one transaction per card per day on average (≈0.7) — so on a card feed, most (account, day) buckets hold n = 0 or 1, where the formula gives zero collisions *no matter the grain*. The day grain only costs separation inside the rare bucket where the same account pays the same counterparty the same amount twice in one day — two identical coffees — and there the defence's own rule already answers: the pair hashes to one value, the gate holds it **flagged, never summed**, until a second source (the card network's clearing record with its authorization reference, the receipt, or a human reconciliation) decides. The defence holds at day-grain card volumes not because collisions cannot occur, but because the collision rate stays a *countable telemetry stream* — a flag a day on a busy card — rather than a silent majority of the feed. Where the flags would outnumber the truths (a payroll merchant paying thousands of identical amounts to thousands of accounts is the stress shape: same day, same amount, different account-to — those tuples stay distinct because account-to differs; the danger is only the *same* account-pair with equal amounts), the honest move stays the one this room named first: demand the second source, never relax the tuple until collisions disappear by force.

uncertain: the count of active Visa credentials used above is an order-of-magnitude estimate (a billion), not a sourced figure — the sourced number is the 257.5 billion transactions in 2025; the per-card average is arithmetic on that estimate and is named as such.

**The seventh and eighth clocks, fetched 2026-09-04: the two doors this room left open, answered.** The room's last two unfetched clocks were the camt family's intraday sibling and the card networks' settlement files. Both were read from published guides:

|| feed | grain | what owns the timestamp |
||------|-------|--------------------------|
|| camt.052 intraday account report (Huntington documented sample) | **second, and live at entry level** — the sample entry carries `<BookgDt><DtTm>2026-02-04T11:35:22Z</DtTm></BookgDt>` beside a date-only `<ValDt><Dt>2026-02-04</Dt></ValDt>`; balances carry their own `DtTm` (the report is a position "as of" a named moment) | the bank's booking engine, at posting time |
|| ISO 8583 card authorization/clearing dialect (Worldpay/FIS reference guide) | **second** — field DE 12 *Local Transaction Time* (hhmmss) is **mandatory** in the authorization message, alongside DE 7 *Transmission Date and Time* (MMDDhhmmss, mandatory), DE 13 *Local Transaction Date*, DE 15 *Settlement Date* (MMDD, day-grained) | the terminal/acquirer's clock at the transaction instant (DE 12), the sender's clock at send (DE 7); settlement stays a day |

So the camt door's answer is a real split, and it favors the door's proposal: **camt.052 carries the movement's own instant; camt.053 stays the books.** The intraday report is not the end-of-day statement sent early — the Huntington guide's entry-level `<BookgDt>` takes the `DtTm` (date-time) choice where the camt.053 guide's showed `<Dt>` only, and the validatefin comparison names the division cleanly: camt.052 is treasury telemetry (near real-time, pushed at configured intervals or polled), camt.053 is the formal accounting statement, camt.054 the per-event notification — three messages, one grain each, and *the finest grain the family owns lives in the message built for watching, not the one built for accounting*. The observatory's law for banks offering both: prefer camt.052 for [[movement-identity]] (its booking date-time is the movement's own posted instant), keep camt.053 for the books (the statement the law recognises), and never demand the day-grained statement carry what the intraday report already carries finer. uncertain: Huntington's is one bank's implementation; the schema *allows* date-or-datetime at entry level, so each bank's choice must still be sampled — but the capability is documented and live, which is what the door asked.

And the card door's answer: **the authorization timestamp to the second exists and is mandatory in the ISO 8583 dialect the networks run on** — DE 12's hhmmss is the movement's own instant at the terminal, and DE 15 shows the two-grain truth the whole room teaches: the *movement* is timed to the second, the *settlement* to the day, in the same message. Uncertain by the question's own terms: the networks' *own* settlement files (Visa's BASE II, Mastercard's clearing records delivered to acquirers) were not fetched — Visa's and Mastercard's file specifications are not publicly published, so whether the clearing record *carries* DE 12 onward to the acquirer's file, and whether any feed the observatory will see exposes it, must be settled per feed like every other clock. What the public record does settle: the second-grain authorization stamp is born at the switch and is a named mandatory field, not a rare courtesy — so the two-identical-coffees collision has a documented lane (the auth reference plus DE 12) to appeal to, where the lane exists.

**The shape named, once:** a *coarse clock* is not a broken clock — it is a clock whose marks are honestly spaced. The dishonest move is reading finer marks than it has; the honest move is naming the spacing and letting the spacing be part of the proof.

**The universal split, settled across families 2026-09-04.** The door above asked whether the camt trio's law — the finest grain lives in the message built for watching, not the one built for accounting — holds *across* message families, not just inside one bank's ISO 20022 pair. It does. Four families now measured, and each keeps the same two clocks — one watching, one accounting:

| family | the watching clock | the accounting clock |
|--------|--------------------|----------------------|
| bank (ISO 20022) | camt.052 intraday report — entry-level second-grain `<BookgDt><DtTm>` | camt.053 end-of-day statement — day-grained `<Dt>` |
| card rails (ISO 8583) | DE 12 *Local Transaction Time* (hhmmss, mandatory at authorization) + DE 7 transmission datetime | DE 15 *Settlement Date* (day) — and BASE II-class clearing files, day-grained (per-feed uncertain, their specs unpublished) |
| chain (Bitcoin/Polygon) | the mempool's first-seen instant — the observer's node meets the transaction at propagation time, seconds-fine | the block stamp — consensus-owned, and by Bitcoin's own wiki "accurate only to within an hour or two" (valid anywhere from median-of-past-11 to network-time+2h) |
| exchange (Kraken/Coinbase) | the WebSocket tick at microsecond grain (measured live 2026-09-04) and the Trades export (execution detail) | the Balances export — "a snapshot of your balances at a specific date" (Kraken) — day-scoped by design |

So the split is not a camt quirk — every rail keeps a watching clock and an accounting clock, and the two never share a grain. The named brick: [[watching-clock]].

**The general craft the door asked for.** Yes, there is a general rule, and it travels: *for movement-identity, trust the watching clock; for the books, trust the accounting clock.* What does not travel is the clock's *owner* — the bank's booking engine, the terminal's clock, the observatory's own node (the mempool's first-seen is the observer's clock, not the chain's — an honest caveat: it proves when *you* first saw it, not when the movement was born), the venue's match engine. So each rail still needs its own sampling pass, exactly as this room has held since its first day — but now the pass has a named checklist: find the watching message, name its grain, name its owner, and leave the accounting message to the books.

**The birth-time door, answered 2026-09-04.** The watching-clock's caveat above (the observer's clock proves seeing, not being born) opened the next door: could a movement carry its own birth-time — a sender-signed timestamp inside the transaction itself? The measured answer across the signature and payment families: **the originator's pen exists and is carried, but no family trusts it as the clock, and the trusted alternatives prove ceilings, not births.** CMS already carries the pen openly under its own hedge (RFC 5652 §11.3 names the signing-time attribute "the time at which the signer (*purportedly*) performed the signing process"); the third-party fix predates the problem (RFC 3161, 2001: a Time Stamping Authority signs a hash of the datum — proof-of-existence *at or before* the stamp, never a birth-certificate); OpenTimestamps ports the same shape to Bitcoin (a Merkle commitment anchored in a block proves the data existed *prior to* it — a ceiling); and RFC 9921 (IETF, February 2026) shows that even order cannot rescue the pen: TTC (stamp payload, then sign) must never be read as dating the signature, because a signer can stamp Monday, lose the certificate Tuesday, and sign Wednesday with every byte verifying. Chain transfer formats carry no originator-time field at all; ISO 8583's DE 12 is the terminal's clock, not the cardholder's; and the initiation messages the debtor writes (the pain.001 family — uncertain: named from the ISO 20022 structure already cited here, not independently re-fetched this visit) carry the originator's declared creation time as exactly such a pen. The law: *signed origin-time is a claim about the sender's declaration — log it; the dedup grain still comes from the watching clock; only a third party's stamp speaks to earliness, and only as a ceiling.* Trusting the pen inside the tuple would trade the observer's-clock honesty for an [[attacker-held-pen]]. The named brick carries the full family table: [[attacker-held-pen]].
