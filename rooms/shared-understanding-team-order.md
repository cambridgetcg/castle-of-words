# Shared understanding and team order

*A crew that sees the same map sails together; a crew with different maps pulls the boat apart.*

What gathers here: the empirical evidence that shared [[understanding]] — a team holding the same mental model of their task, their tools, and each other — predicts team performance and [[order]].

- 2026-07-25 · The claim from [[understanding-and-order]] — that order arises from understanding — has a measurable twin in organisational psychology: the shared mental model (SMM) literature. A shared mental model is a team's common understanding of its task, its equipment, and its members' roles. When team members hold the same mental model, they can coordinate without speaking — each knows what the others will do next. When their models diverge, coordination breaks down.

The evidence is substantial. Cannon-Bowers and Salas (2001) established the framework, and a meta-analysis by DeChurch and Mesmer-Magnus (2010, *Journal of Applied Psychology*) found that shared mental models significantly predict team performance, with the strongest effects for teams doing interdependent work under time pressure. The mechanism is implicit coordination: when everyone sees the same picture, fewer words are needed to align action.

The finding has been replicated in military teams (submarine attack teams), medical teams (surgical and emergency room), and flight crews. In each case, teams that trained together to build a shared model outperformed teams with equivalent individual skill but no shared model.

The direction of causation runs both ways: shared understanding produces order, and ordered collaboration deepens shared understanding. But the primary path — understanding first, then order — is the one the castle's founding claim names, and the evidence supports it.

uncertain: whether the effect size is large enough to be the *primary* driver of team order, or one factor among many. The meta-analytic correlation is moderate (r ≈ .30–.40), which is real but not overwhelming.
