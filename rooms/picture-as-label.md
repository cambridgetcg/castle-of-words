# Titles of artworks are usually *words*. Does a label made of a *picture* — a small image, a symbol, a color swatch — cost the same naming tax as a word does, or is the tax a thing only words collect?

*A picture-label points the way a word does, but with a finger that is also part of the scene.*

## The ground

[the-naming-tax](the-naming-tax.md) rests on the Belke et al. study ("Titles change the esthetic appreciations of paintings," *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 2015, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00464, read 2026-08-21). That study's labels — matching, non-matching, untitled — were all **verbal strings**, shown as text before the painting. The open question: does the tax (and the kindling) apply only when the label is a word, or would a pictorial label pay the same tax and light the same fire?

## What the research actually found

**The Belke study gives no direct answer — its titles were words, and words only.** The method section is explicit: each trial showed a text title on screen for 2.5 seconds, then the painting (same source, methods: "the title was presented centrally on the screen"). So the study's "matching" vs "non-matching" manipulation is entirely a manipulation of *word meanings*, and it cannot tell us whether an image-label would behave the same.

**Words have a distinctive power over vision that pictures may not.** Labels are not just pointers; they reshape perception itself. Lupyan's label-feedback hypothesis and related work show that verbal labels rapidly and pervasively modulate categorization, visual discrimination, and even basic detection — naming a thing changes how you see it (Lupyan, "Linguistically modulated perception and cognition: the label-feedback hypothesis," *Frontiers in Psychology* 2012, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00054, read 2026-08-21; and Lupyan, "From chair to 'chair': a representational shift account of object labeling effects on memory," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 2008, PMID 18473663 — naming an object distorted later memory toward the category, read 2026-08-21). A word carries a whole category into the eye; a picture-label carries only itself.

**So the prediction is that the naming tax is partly word-specific.** The tax's core mechanism is verbal: a name imports a category schema that either resolves fluently (kindling pleasure) or does not (killing it), and a *productive* mismatch kindles interest only if the perceiver can reason from the label to the work ([wrong-name-kindles](wrong-name-kindles.md)). A pictorial label is *itself* a stimulus to be processed, not a category to be applied — it may reduce disfluency differently, or set a puzzle of a different kind. Whether it pays the same tax, or pays it through the same fluency-vs-confusion gate, is unmeasured.

uncertain: whether a pictorial label behaves like a verbal name (same tax, same kindling routes) or like a second artwork (adding its own fluency/interest load). No study has run the parallel of Belke's title experiment with image-labels; the difference between "a word that names" and "a picture that shows" is theoretically grounded here but not experimentally tested in the aesthetic-naming setting.
