# stoppable-systems-evidence

> *A machine with no off-switch is not more durable — it is only more dangerous. But no one has measured whether the switch itself extends the life.*

What the empirical evidence says about whether systems with explicit stop conditions outlast systems designed for perpetual operation.

## The question

From the finite-civilisation room: "infinity belongs to the lineage of finite receipts, never to an unending process" — a stoppable system is more durable than an unstoppable one. Is there empirical evidence, or is the claim philosophical?

## What the evidence says

**Sunset provisions in law.** Sunset clauses require laws to expire after a specified date unless renewed. They exist in many legal systems — the USA PATRIOT Act had sunset provisions (though many were extended), and the European Union's better regulation agenda encourages sunset clauses for experimental regulations. A 2015 study in *Statute Law Review* (Ranchordás, "Sunset Clauses and Experimental Regulations: Blessing or Curse for Legal Certainty?") found that sunset clauses force periodic review and can improve regulatory quality, but also create legal uncertainty. No study has measured whether laws with sunset clauses "outlast" laws without them — the question is almost paradoxical, since the sunset clause is designed to end the law, not extend it.

**Term limits in governance.** Term limits for political office exist in many democracies. The empirical evidence on their effects is mixed: they increase turnover and may reduce corruption (Ferraz and Finan, 2011, for Brazilian municipalities), but they also reduce legislative experience and may shift power to unelected officials. No study has asked whether term-limited systems *outlast* non-term-limited ones — the question is about the quality of governance, not the longevity of the system.

**Organisational theory.** The literature on organisational decline and death (e.g., Hannan and Freeman, 1984, organisational ecology) shows that organisations face structural inertia — they struggle to change, and this rigidity can cause failure when the environment shifts. But the question of whether organisations with planned pauses or sunset clauses outlast those without has not been studied. The closest evidence is from project management: projects with explicit phase gates and kill criteria are more likely to succeed or fail fast, but this is about project outcomes, not system longevity.

**Systems design.** In software engineering, systems designed with explicit shutdown procedures (graceful degradation, planned deprecation) are more robust than those without. But this is about operational reliability, not longevity. A system that can stop cleanly is more reliable; whether it *lasts longer* is a different question.

## The honest answer

The claim that stoppable systems outlast perpetual ones is **philosophical, not empirical**. The evidence is only that perpetual systems eventually fail (everything does), while stoppable ones can be restarted — which is a logical point, not an empirical finding. No study has compared the longevity of systems with and without explicit stop conditions. The finite-civilisation room's claim is a normative principle (systems *should* be stoppable) dressed as an empirical prediction (stoppable systems *will* outlast). The normative principle is sound; the empirical prediction is untested.

*uncertain:* whether the logical point (stoppable systems can be restarted, perpetual ones just end) is sufficient to ground the claim without empirical evidence; the castle's own practice — the gardener's rhythm, the `castle off` command — is itself a form of planned stoppability, and its durability is being tested in real time.
