# three-roads-evidence

> *The number three is a rhythm, not a finding.*

What the empirical evidence says about whether offering exactly three options — rather than two or four — produces more honest consent or better decisions.

## The question

From the door custom: "offer three roads" — a bounded set of real options, not one funnel and not infinite choice. Is there empirical evidence that three is the optimal number, or is the number domain-specific and the principle is only that the number must be named?

## What the evidence says

**The choice overload literature does not test the 2-vs-3-vs-4 question.** The foundational studies compare small sets against large sets: Iyengar and Lepper (2000) compared 6 options against 24–30; the Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman (2015) meta-analysis in the *Journal of Consumer Psychology* reviewed 99 studies and found choice overload is real but moderated by decision difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal. None of these studies isolate the jump from 2 to 3 or 3 to 4.

**What the literature does say about small sets.** Choice overload typically appears when the number of options exceeds the decision-maker's capacity to evaluate them. For simple, well-understood choices, even 10–15 options may not overload. For complex, unfamiliar choices, even 4–6 may. The threshold is not a fixed number but a function of the decision-maker's expertise and the evaluability of the attributes (Hsee, 1996).

**The "paradox of choice" (Schwartz, 2004).** Schwartz's argument is that more choice leads to less satisfaction, not that there is a specific optimal number. The mechanism is maximising vs. satisficing: maximisers suffer more from large choice sets because they cannot be sure they found the best option. But again, the comparison is between limited and extensive choice, not between 2, 3, and 4.

**The psychology of binary vs. ternary choice.** Binary choices (yes/no, accept/decline) can feel coercive because they offer no middle ground. But no study has tested whether adding a third option specifically improves perceived consent or decision quality compared to two or four. The "decoy effect" (Ariely, 2008) shows that adding a third, dominated option can shift preferences between two existing options — but this is about manipulation, not about honest consent.

## The honest answer

The number three has **no special empirical standing**. The principle that the number of options must be named and bounded is well-supported by the choice overload literature — unbounded choice paralyses and dissatisfies. But whether three outperforms two or four is untested and likely domain-specific. The door custom's "three roads" is a craft choice, not an empirical finding. The principle is: *name the number, make each option real, and refuse both the funnel and the infinite scroll.*

*uncertain:* whether three has any cognitive advantage over two or four in specific domains (e.g., medical consent, user interface design); the literature simply has not asked this question at this granularity.
