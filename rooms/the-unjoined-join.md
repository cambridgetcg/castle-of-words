# The Unjoined Join

*Two instruments stand on the same workbench, each built by another hand; nobody has yet clamped them together.*

[[the-light-cargo]] found the nearest reading to the door's pristine test: Upworthy's thousands of headline A/B field experiments, where professional writers wrote variant headlines for the same article and the platform randomly assigned them. But the Upworthy variants are *professional rewrites*, not organic mutations — no cascade of copying hands acted on them. The door this room opens asks the next thing: **could the Upworthy archive's variant-versus-variant outcomes be joined to mutation-edit-distance between the paired headlines, giving the first survival-versus-edit-direction reading on cargo with no hunter — and would the memorability-features regression (word frequency, concreteness, emotionality norms on the winning variants) then ride real selection, not just A/B assignment?** Read 2026-08-24.

**The answer, in one breath.** The join is feasible in principle and unbuilt in fact. Both instruments exist and are publicly downloadable: the Upworthy Research Archive (Matias, Gligorić, West & Lifchits 2021, Nature Scientific Data, doi:10.1038/s41597-021-00934-7) carries every A/B test Upworthy ran from 2013–2015 — the variants, the impressions, the clicks, the winner per test. Simmons, Adamic & Adar's quotation-mutation pipeline (ICWSM 2011) is published and was written for exactly the edit-distance half: extracting quoted-text families, measuring mutation rates between variants, regressing outcomes on linguistic features. No citing paper has yet run one against the other.

**The archive is real and reachable.** Matias, Gligorić, West & Lifchits (2021) published the Upworthy Research Archive as a data descriptor: every A/B field experiment Upworthy ran on headline variants for the same article, with variant-level impressions, clicks, and click-through rates, plus the text of each variant. The dataset is hosted and downloadable; the Gligorić 2023 confirmatory study was built on it, as were several citing papers (OpenAlex citing-set read 2026-08-24: 14 works, none joining mutation edit-distance). ("The Upworthy Research Archive, a time series of 32,487 experiments in U.S. media", Scientific Data 2021, doi:10.1038/s41597-021-00934-7.)

**The Simmons pipeline exists and was written for this.** Simmons, Adamic & Adar (2011) built the original mutation-measurement instrument: they extracted quotation families from millions of news sites and blogs, computed edit-distance between variants within a family, classified mutations (insertions, deletions, substitutions), and measured how variant frequency changed over time. The pipeline was written for organic cascades, but the instrument is general: take any family of text variants, measure pairwise edit-distances, and regress each variant's success on the distance to its parent and on word-level features (frequency, concreteness, emotionality norms). ("Memes Online: Extracted, Subtracted, Injected, and Recollected", ICWSM 2011; OpenAlex W76678209.)

**What the join would settle.** The Upworthy archive's limitation, stated plainly in the parent room, is that its variants were written by professionals *before* publication — no *post-hoc* cascade selected them. The Gligorić effect sizes (negative emotion β = 0.18, third-person pronouns β = 0.22, length β = 0.07) are therefore pre-selection effects: what wins under random assignment, not what survives under copying. The join would close that gap in one direction: for each A/B test, compute the *pairwise edit distance* between the winning variant and each losing variant, then regress *win probability* on word-level features of the winner — frequency, concreteness, emotionality — controlling for test-level covariates. That would give the first survival-versus-edit-direction reading on cargo where *no hunter was acting* (no moderator, no algorithmic filter, no adversary). unc​ertain: whether within-test effect sizes are large enough to detect word-level norms against the noise of professional rewriting; the pairs were written for distinctness, not drift.

**What no one has done.** A full-text search of all 14 works citing Gligorić 2023 (OpenAlex, read 2026-08-24) found none that ran mutation edit-distance against the archive's variant pairs: the citing papers use the archive for headline-click prediction, curiosity-gap studies, or media framing — none compute pairwise edit distance between the variants within a test. The joining study is buildable from standing parts, unbuilt.

---

**The calibration half: has anyone checked whether A/B winners mutate differently once released?** (2026-08-24 · gardener's reading of OpenAlex) The door asked whether the constant-per-copy law survives platform selection — whether a winning headline, pre-hardened by A/B testing, resists mutation once released into the wild, or mutates at the same rate as the losers. The short answer: **nobody has run that check.** A full pass through the 30 works citing the Upworthy archive (OpenAlex, read 2026-08-24) found none that tracked post-release mutation of winning versus losing variants. The citing papers fall into four camps — predictors of click-through (negativity, identity, simplicity), methodology (statistical challenges of online experiments), ethics (informed consent in A/B testing), and tooling (LLM-assisted headline generation). None treat the archive as material for the mutation question. The Simmons lineage (Simmons, Adamic & Adar 2011) *did* measure mutation rates between quotation variants across millions of blogs and news sites — but the variants it tracked were the ones that already existed in the wild, not the ones a platform had pre-selected in a laboratory. The two halves of the needed test are both built and both public; the joining study is unbuilt.

**What the literature does say about winners in the wild.** The closest evidence is indirect but suggestive: Robertson et al. (2023) used the Upworthy archive itself to show that negative-bias in news consumption is driven more by news *creators* than *consumers* ("Negativity drives online news consumption", Nature Human Behaviour 2023, doi:10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4 — read via OpenAlex 2026-08-24). And the broader citation ring around the archive includes work on what makes content shareable in the wild (Berger & Milkman 2012; emotional virality, arousal, identity) — but always with content as *given*, never with winners-versus-losers as the independent variable. The A/B winner is the product of the platform's choice; nobody has yet asked whether that choice changes the cargo's mutability. **uncertain:** whether the winner's pre-hardening is even measurable outside the archive — the test would need an organic cascade with the same headlines, which the archive does not collect.

**Status.** Open: the calibration study (A/B winners tracked for mutation resistance in organic sharing) has not been run; the join itself remains unbuilt; the winner-mutation calibration check is a third unbuilt study neither the Upworthy archive nor the Simmons pipeline alone can deliver.
