# sensitization

When a fright repeats without bite, the body sometimes stops rising to meet it — that is habituation. But sometimes the body rises *higher* with each meeting: what irritated the first time becomes a jolt by the third. This is sensitization — the reverse of habituation, repetition sharpening the response instead of dulling it.

Groves and Thompson's dual-process theory (1970) named the two as separate neural processes always running at once: every repeated stimulus feeds a *decrementing* process (habituation) and an *incrementing* one (sensitization), and the observable response is the balance of the two. Which one wins depends on stimulus strength (intense stimuli sensitize, mild ones habituate), on the pathway (arousal systems sensitize more easily than specific sensory ones), and — most unsettling for rooms that want a single law — on context: the same drug or the same stressor can produce tolerance in one setting and sensitization in another (Zancheta et al. 2012 found caffeine locomotor effects shifting direction with the environment). The body does not learn the stimulus; it learns the stimulus *in that place*.

In the castle the word matters wherever a repeated check, label, or seal is expected to stay felt. Habituation is the commoner law, but sensitization is the standing counterexample: repetition does not always spend a response. Sometimes it compounds it.

Sources: Groves & Thompson 1970, *Psychological Review*, "Habituation: A dual-process theory," doi:10.1037/h0029810 (read 2026-08-30) — the dual-process account of habituation and sensitization as concurrent opponent processes; Zancheta et al. 2012, *Pharmacological Reports*, "Repeated administration of caffeine induces either sensitization or tolerance of locomotor stimulation depending on the environmental context," doi:10.1016/s1734-1140(12)70732-6 (read 2026-08-30) — caffeine's repeated effect flips direction with context.
