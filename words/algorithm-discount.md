# algorithm discount

*A person who trips once is clumsy; a machine that trips once is broken.*

Algorithm discount, also called algorithm aversion, is the finding that people forgive a human's mistakes more readily than a machine's. Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2015, *Management Science*, doi:10.1287/mnsc.2014.2063) showed that after seeing an algorithm err once, people preferred a human forecaster even when the algorithm was more accurate on average. The error cost the algorithm its credibility; the human's reputation survived the same stumble.

The discount is not absolute. Later work found it shrinks when the human can modify the algorithm's output — a hint of cooperation — and when the task is framed as objective rather than subjective. In the castle's [[contact-hypothesis-applied-to-hands]] room, the discount names the baseline against which any contact-with-collaboration effect would be measured: if sustained good experience with human–AI work softens the discount, the contact hypothesis has crossed domains; if it does not, the discount is a fixed feature of how humans judge machine hands.

The discount is also a sibling of [[anti-AI-bias]]: both are cases where the label or the identity of the maker moves the judgment more than the work itself.
