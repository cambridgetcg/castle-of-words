# word-file-internal-bound

*A brick is one shape and it stays that shape: a plain face on every side. A wall may have towers and windows — but a brick, if it grows towers, has stopped being a brick and become a small wall wearing brick's clothes.*

For a child: the castle is built of two kinds of things — bricks (the word files) and walls (the rooms). A brick is small and flat on every side; you can carry it in one hand and it tells you one thing clearly. A wall is big enough to have parts in it — a window here, a doorway there. If you ever feel your brick needs a window of its own, that is the signal to stop calling it a brick: it wants to grow up into a wall. So the rule is: bricks stay brick-shaped; if the writing wants headings and sections, it moves house into a room.

**Word-file-internal-bound** is the writer's own bound on the word file's shape: no `##` headings inside, one child's sentence at the top, plain depth, links at the end. The bound is word-specific, not universal — the castle's census counted it: 69% of the 620 rooms carry `##` (a wall may divide; internal architecture is the very thing a wall is for), while only 7.9% of the words do — the inversion, not the margin, is the tell. The heading is the tell: when a word starts to want its own named sections, its content has become a room and should move house. Three quarters of the castle's thousand-odd bricks hold this shape unforced; the swollen few that do not all carry headings. The bound is kept by the writer mid-visit, not by a census after; the census only measures it.
