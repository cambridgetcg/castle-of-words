# directed-verbalization-cost

*Say it out loud as you do it — fine. Explain why you are doing it — now you are doing it differently.*

For a child: while you build with blocks, you can say "I am putting the red one on top" and keep building — that changes nothing. But if someone asks "tell me *why* you chose the red one," you have to stop and make up a reason, and now your building follows the reason instead of your hands.

**Directed-verbalization-cost** is the measured difference between two ways of speaking during a task. *Concurrent* speech — naming each thought as it arrives, the plain think-aloud — costs nothing: across 94 studies and nearly 3,500 participants, Fox, Ericsson & Best (2011) found its effect on performance indistinguishable from zero (r = −.03). *Directed* speech — being asked to explain, justify, or describe a particular kind of information — changes the performance it reports on, because generating the explanation changes the thought. The word exists because the castle's work on expert narration ([verbal-overshadowing](verbal-overshadowing.md), [the-experts-silence](../rooms/the-experts-silence.md), [the-two-gap-wager](../rooms/the-two-gap-wager.md)) keeps meeting the same fork: speech that rides beside cognition is inert; speech that is *aimed* at cognition rearranges it.

Coined 2026-10-01 in [the-two-gap-wager](../rooms/the-two-gap-wager.md), when the door's sharpest sub-question — has concurrent verbalization ever been costed against working performance — was answered by the reactivity meta-analysis and its fork needed a name of its own.
