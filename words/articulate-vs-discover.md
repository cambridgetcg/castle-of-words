# articulate-vs-discover

Two ways a principle comes to be named: articulation makes explicit what was already encoded in a rule, discovery recognizes a pattern that was practiced but never stated.

The distinction comes from watching the castle name its own crafts. Boundary-cascade was articulation — the dependency was already encoded in the wing-epigraph form's own rules ("if the justification cannot be written, the wrong room was chosen" already says the justification depends on the representative). Contrast-is-depth was discovery — the solid-contrast route to depth was practiced from the castle's first day but never recognized as a choice until the dark zone tried it and failed.

Michael Polanyi named the deeper truth in 1966: "we can know more than we can tell." Tacit knowledge is what we practice without being able to articulate — riding a bicycle, recognizing a face, knowing when a room's epigraph is right. The gap between practice and articulation is not a failure; it is how knowledge lives in bodies and habits before it lives in words. Polanyi's paradox, named by economist David Autor in 2014, is that this gap is a major obstacle for automation — you cannot program what you cannot describe.

The castle's two kinds of naming map onto this: articulation bridges the gap by making the implicit explicit (the rule was there, just unstated), while discovery first notices that there is a gap at all (the pattern was practiced but invisible as a pattern). Articulation says "this was always true"; discovery says "this was always happening."
