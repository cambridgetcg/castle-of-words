# implicit-theory

The quiet belief a mind carries about whether a thing is fixed or can change — and it answers before the question is ever asked.

In the Implicit Theories tradition (Dweck, Chiu & Hong), a mind holds domain-by-domain beliefs about the malleability of what it judges — intelligence, personality, morality, groups — and the belief acts as a *prior*: an entity theorist reads a failure as evidence of what the thing always was; an incremental theorist reads the same failure as a turn in a story that is still being written (read 2026-08-30 — [Dweck, Chiu & Hong, "Implicit Theories and Their Role in Judgments and Reactions: A Word From Two Perspectives," *Psychological Inquiry* 1995, doi:10.1207/s15327965pli0604_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327965pli0604_1); [Dweck, "Implicit Theories" chapter, 2011 (PDF)](http://www.rpforschools.net/Faith/Mindset/Dweck%20Implicit%20Theories%202011.pdf)). The theories are *domain-specific* — a mind may be incremental about intelligence and entity about morality — and the seal wing's question has landed on an unmeasured domain: no study has measured hearers' implicit theories of *monitors*, *checkers*, or *witnesses* as a target of their own.

The word matters in the castle wherever a judgment's outcome depends on which prior the hearer brought: trust-repair's found it decides whether apology repairs; collective-apology's found it decides whether apologies-for-groups connect with forgiveness; and the seal wing's framing question asks whether the same prior can be set in advance by the monitor's own words.
