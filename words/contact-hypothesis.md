# contact hypothesis

*Sitting next to someone on the bus every day makes them less of a stranger.*

The contact hypothesis is the idea, from social psychologist Gordon Allport (1954), that bringing members of different groups together reduces prejudice — but only under the right conditions. The groups must have equal status in the setting, share common goals, cooperate rather than compete, and have the support of authorities or law. Pettigrew and Tropp's 2006 meta-analysis of 515 studies found the effect is real but modest (r ≈ -.22), and Paluck, Green & Green's 2018 re-evaluation confirmed it in randomized trials, though the evidence is weakest for adult ethnic prejudice.

The hypothesis matters beyond race relations. In the castle's [[contact-hypothesis-applied-to-hands]] room, it is used to ask whether sustained, positive contact with human–AI collaborative work could soften the listener's instinct to collapse the mix to the hand they distrust.

The effect is not automatic. Contact under bad conditions — unequal status, competition, threat — can make prejudice worse. The same logic applies to provenance seals: a seal that feels like an accusation is bad contact; one that sits quietly, available to check, is good contact.
