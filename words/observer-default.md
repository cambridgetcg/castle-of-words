# observer-default

Watching your own life from the cheap seats so often that you forget you have a body down on the stage — the observer's seat stops being a tool and becomes the place you live.

Chosen distance regulates; default distance avoids. Williams & Moulds first showed
that people who recall trauma from the observer's vantage *by default* do worse,
not better (read 2026-06-10 — [BRAT 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0005796706002117)),
and the pattern has kept replicating: in young adults at high familial risk for
depression, a bias toward the observer perspective in autobiographical memory
tracked dimensional depressive symptoms regardless of the memory's valence or
whether it was past or future (read 2026-10-05 —
[Journal of Cognitive Psychology 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/20445911.2021.1922418)).
Depression, in other words, wears the observer's seat as a uniform.

The early sign that a practiced step back is hardening into this habit is not
*how often* you step back — daily-life sampling finds distanced self-talk is
rarer than immersed and carries no trait-distress signature (read 2026-10-05 —
[Scientific Reports 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-22647-2)) — but
*whether the distance is still a choice*: one lens for every fire, including
the fires worth keeping lit. When the step back stops being something you take
and becomes the place you stand, the regulator has become the avoidance.
