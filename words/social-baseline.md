# social-baseline

The brain treats a trusted companion as a metabolic resource — a load it does not have to carry alone — and the body's alarm runs cooler when the hand it knows is near.

Social Baseline Theory (Coan, Beckes) holds that the brain's default state is social, not solitary: the brain expects to share the metabolic cost of vigilance with trusted others, and when a trusted partner is present, the threat system does not mount the full response. Coan's handholding studies (fMRI) showed partner presence reduced amygdala activation during a mild shock threat, with the effect scaling by relationship quality — the closer the partner, the lower the threat response. The theory reframes the partner not as a scaffold (temporary support to be faded) but as a bioenergetic resource (a permanent subsidy the brain budgets for). This distinction matters for therapy: if the partner is a resource, fading the partner does not teach self-regulation — it forces the brain to spend more energy, and the skill may not build.

The emotion-regulation wing of the castle has turned to this concept in four rooms. [fading-vs-replacing](../rooms/fading-vs-replacing.md) found Social Baseline Theory says the partner is a resource, not a scaffold, and the replacement needs to be social. [alone-or-accompanied](../rooms/alone-or-accompanied.md) designed the three-condition test (alone, partner-silent, partner-interacting) that would separate the co-regulation subsidy from self-regulation. [solo-or-social-replacement](../rooms/solo-or-social-replacement.md) found the replacement splits into three social forms, each with a different mechanism, and the right one depends on whether the deficit is the subsidy or the skill. [general-voice-or-specific-presence](../rooms/general-voice-or-specific-presence.md) found the partner's presence works through prevention (dampening the threat system before it floods) while the trained general voice works through recovery (activating the soothing system after the threat is felt) — two different points on the threat-response arc.
