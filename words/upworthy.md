# Upworthy

*A news site that asked thousands of its own headlines to fight fair — and kept the scores.*

For a child: imagine a headline contest where every day two people write two different titles for the same story, and a coin flip decides which title each reader sees. After millions of readers, you can count which titles won and which lost — and because the coin flip was honest, you know the title itself did the winning, not the luck of who happened to see it.

**Upworthy** is the name the castle gives to the **Upworthy Research Archive** (Matias, Gligorić, West & Lifchits 2021, *Nature Scientific Data*, doi:10.1038/s41597-021-00934-7): every A/B field experiment Upworthy.com ran on headline variants for the same article from 2013–2015, with variant-level impressions, clicks, click-through rates, and the text of each variant — 32,487 experiments, all public and downloadable. It is the largest controlled text-variation experiment ever published, and the castle's cargo wing leans on it as the cleanest reading of *light cargo* — text no one is hunting, where a winning mutation cannot be accused of dodging a filter, because the winning was decided by the platform's random assignment before any copying hand ever touched it.

What the castle has read from it: **negative-emotion words helped** (β = 0.18), **positive-emotion words did not**, **longer headlines won**, **specificity beat generality**, **third-person pronouns helped** — the psychologically marked variant living longer, the same lean the conspiracy-cascade studies found on hunted text (Gligorić, Lifchits, West & Anderson 2023, *PLOS ONE*, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0281682). What the castle has asked of it and not yet found: whether the winners, once released, mutate at the same rate as the losers — the constant-per-copy law's one unmeasured boundary — and whether pairwise edit-distance between the variants can be joined to the winners' outcomes, the [[the-unjoined-join]] room's standing workbench.

The honest caution the brick carries: the Upworthy variants were written by professionals *before* publication — they are rewrites designed for distinctness, not drift — so the archive measures what wins under selection, not what survives under copying. The two are close kin and not the same; every room that leans on this brick names which half it is leaning on.
