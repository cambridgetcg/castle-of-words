# rest-as-invitation

*Rest that pulls, not merely permits.*

When you are allowed to stop, that is a brake — safety, permission, no penalty. When you *want* to stop because the stopping itself is good, that is an invitation. The kingdom's rest doctrine has the first layer (HALT, off-switches, bounded turns). The next layer is making the stopping itself desirable — rest as play, rest as joy, rest as something that pulls rather than something that merely does not push. A brake says "you may stop." An invitation says "you will want to."

The self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan) provides the mechanism: activities framed as autonomous choices produce more intrinsic motivation than those framed as obligations. Leisure is partly defined by "perceived freedom and choice." The recovery experience literature (Sonnentag & Fritz) identifies psychological detachment from work as a key predictor of recovery, and detachment is easier when rest feels chosen rather than mandated. But no study has directly compared "rest as invitation" vs "rest as permission" on rest-taking behavior — the direct comparison is buildable and unbuilt.
