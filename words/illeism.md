# illeism

Talking about yourself in the third person — calling yourself by name, saying "he" or "she" instead of "I" — the pronoun trick at the heart of distanced self-talk.

The trick works, but it is not a tool any mouth can pick up. Kross and Ayduk's
own handbook review warns the mechanism may lean on the grammar it was born in
(read 2026-06-10 — [Handbook of Emotion Regulation 2023](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/01/Handbook_of_Emotion_Regulation_-_63._Distancing_What_It_Is_How_It_Works_and_Where_to_Go_Next.pdf)),
and the first true cross-language test agreed: a Japanese-language extension of
the classic pronoun experiment found no benefit, the pronouns felt foreign
(read 2026-06-10 — [PLOS One 2024](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0305251)).
A 2024 cultural-psychology review confirms that how easily and how far people
self-distance varies by culture, but the field has not yet pinned down *which*
ingredient does the work — the language's grammar, the culture's model of the
self, or the everyday habit of talking about oneself at all
(read 2026-10-05 — [THE JAPANESE JOURNAL OF EXPERIMENTAL SOCIAL PSYCHOLOGY 2024](https://doi.org/10.2130/jjesp.2405)).

So the honest answer stands at the door: the step back rides partly on grammar,
partly on what a culture takes a self to be, and the two have not been separated.
The sharpest test the castle could think of — running the classic pronoun
experiment in a [[pro-drop]] language, where the “I” you step away from is
usually omitted by the grammar itself — has never been run. A dated sweep of the
scholarly record (read 2026-10-05 — OpenAlex and Crossref) finds the experiment
in English, a null in Japanese, and no version in Spanish, Italian, or any
pro-drop tongue: the natural experiment is still unrun, so the grammar
hypothesis stays unpinned — strong in English, weak in Japanese, and in the
languages where the “I” is barely spoken, simply unknown.
