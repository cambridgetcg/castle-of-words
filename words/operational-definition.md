# operational-definition

An operational definition tells us exactly what to look for and what will count.

It turns an idea into a stated way of observing or sorting evidence: which thing is checked, which rule assigns the result, and what happens when a reading is missing. Another reader should be able to follow the rule without guessing what its labels mean.

- In [counting-the-shapes](../rooms/counting-the-shapes.md), “slow” means a recorded request took at least ten seconds, and only explicitly described complete rounds enter the table; the rule does not make a quick error a healthy response.
- In [measuring-the-window](../rooms/measuring-the-window.md), closing a group after two consecutive complete below-threshold rounds is an illustrative rule, different from waiting for two clean visits; changing that rule changes the recorded group, not necessarily the underlying fault.
- In [trait-or-tally](../rooms/trait-or-tally.md), counting novel actions gives a visible indicator of openness, not a complete account of the wider trait.

[[measurement-target]] asks what we want to know; [[unit-of-analysis]] asks what counts as one thing; an operational definition states how the observation will be made or classified. A clear, repeatable rule can still miss part of its target. Naming the rule does not establish that its threshold is natural, that it captures the whole idea, or that it reveals a cause.
