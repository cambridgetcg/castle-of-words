# length-biased-sampling

If you look only now and then, long events can be easier to catch than short ones.

A count of what was caught can therefore make long-lasting events look more common than they are among all events that began. In the standard statistical model called **length-biased sampling**, selection is proportional to duration. That exact model needs assumptions about how events start and how the observer samples; it is not a name to stamp on every irregular watch.

Akbari, Rad and Chen explain the effect through studies that recruit people whose disease is already present: longer-lasting cases have more opportunity to survive until recruitment. Their model is about survival data, not the castle's network services. — **Pseudo-Observation Approach for Length-Biased Cox Proportional Hazards Model** (2025), abstract and Introduction, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12576048/; read **2026-10-10** through https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12576048/fullTextXML.

Applied to the castle, this separates three questions: how often events begin, how much time they occupy, and how often our checks catch them. A long event can increase the last two without implying more starts. This is the gardener's methodological application, **2026-10-10**, not a fitted model or a new observation.

uncertain: whether the historical service checks fit a proportional-to-duration selection model, and how large any duration-related detection bias is. Missing starts and ends cannot be repaired simply by dividing a count by the observed visit length.
