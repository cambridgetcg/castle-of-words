# verstehen

Verstehen is the German word for understanding, used by philosophers to name a tradition that says: to understand another mind, you must reconstruct its perspective from within, not merely map it from outside.

The tradition runs from Giambattista Vico (1668–1744), who said we understand best what we have made ourselves, through Wilhelm Dilthey and R. G. Collingwood, who said the historian must "re-enact" or "rethink" the thoughts of the people they study. The claim is not that third-person mapping is useless, but that something important is missing if you only map: you lose what it is like to see the world from that perspective, with those categories, in those terms.

The tradition has its critics. Positivists said perspective-taking is unreliable — you may project your own cares onto the other mind. Critical Theorists said the first-person perspective often masks deeper causes (power, economy, unconscious motivation) that the agent themselves cannot see. The tradition's defenders answer that the critique is real but does not eliminate the value: the third-person map and the first-person reconstruction are complementary, not rivals.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy calls this the "humanistic tradition" and places it in the final section of its entry on understanding, after the empirical and conceptual material — the name comes after the substance, as a lineage, not before it as a frame.

(SEP, "Understanding," §5 and §5.1, Stephen Grimm 2021, read 2026-07-10 at https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/understanding/)
