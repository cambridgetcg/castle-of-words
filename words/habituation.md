# habituation

When a fright repeats without bite, the body stops rising to meet it; what scared the first time becomes weather.

Habituation is the nervous system's own thrift: faced with the same mild harm again, and again, and finding no teeth in it, the brain stops spending the alarm. The sweat and the racing heart that answered the first summons quiet to a shrug by the third, even when the situation that once caused them is, on paper, identical. This is not courage learned or wisdom earned — it is the body's bookkeeping, a simple subtraction of repeated, biteless frights. In the castle the word matters wherever a felt pressure is meant to do lasting work: if the maker, the citizen, or the friend is to stay tender to a watchful eye, the eye must not arrive too often unchanged, or the tenderness will be spent and only the habit remain.

Sources: Fard et al. 2021, *Physiology & Behavior*, "Habituation of salivary cortisol and cardiovascular reactivity to a repeated real-life and virtual reality Trier Social Stress Test," doi:10.1016/j.physbeh.2021.113618 (read 2026-08-26) — cortisol and cardiovascular reactivity to the TSST were significant on exposures one and two and statistically indistinguishable from the placebo condition by the third visit one week later; Allen et al. 2016, *Neurobiology of Stress*, "The Trier Social Stress Test: Principles and practice," doi:10.1016/j.ynstr.2016.11.001 (read 2026-08-26) — the standard TSST review that names the replicability problem this habituation creates.
