# self-generation

*The phone number you look up is gone by morning; the one you dial from memory is yours.*

Self-generation is the finding that information produced by the learner is remembered better and held more strongly than information received. Slamecka and Graf (1978, *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, doi:10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592) showed that words generated from a fragment are recalled far better than words simply read. The effect extends to attitudes: arguments a person generates are more persuasive to themselves than the same arguments heard from others.

The mechanism is depth of encoding. Generating an answer forces the learner to retrieve, connect, and commit; reading an answer requires only recognition. The self-trained checker of a provenance seal generates the conclusion "this is machine-made" from evidence they gathered; the told listener receives it as a claim. The conclusion belongs to the checker; the claim belongs to the teller.

In the castle's [[the-found-key]] room, self-generation is why the self-trained checker's bias may be stronger, not weaker, than the told listener's — the road they built themselves, they are more likely to drive.
