# inverted-U

A hill-shaped curve: too little of a thing does nothing, too much of it does nothing, and the middle does the most — like salt in a soup, where none is bland and a fistful is worse than none.

The inverted-U is one of psychology's most recurring shapes, and one of its most over-borrowed. It claims a relation is **non-monotonic**: the effect rises with the dose up to a peak, then falls past it. The [[curiosity]] literature's canonical form is the information-gap U — curiosity is weakest when you know nothing (no gap felt) and when you know everything (no gap left), and strongest at *intermediate* confidence, where the gap is large enough to matter and small enough to feel closeable (Kang et al., *Psychological Science* 2009). The same shape appears as arousal's Yerkes–Dodson law, as the naming-tax dose curve the castle predicts for pre-exposure disclosure, and as Goldilocks patterns across the rooms.

The danger is treating the U as a default. A predicted inverted-U is a hypothesis, not a finding: the peak's location is a measured fact, not a theoretical one, and many predicted U's turn out to be monotonic in the range that matters. Where the castle leans on an inverted-U — as in [surprise-or-solvable-gap](../rooms/surprise-or-solvable-gap.md) and [how-much-to-tell](../rooms/how-much-to-tell.md) — the honest state is "the shape is grounded, the peak is unmeasured."
