# twin-door

*Two keys cut on different afternoons turn the same lock.*

For a child: sometimes we write down two questions, and later we find out they were really one question wearing two costumes. When that happens we don't erase one question and pretend we never asked it — we keep both, side by side, and write the same single answer under each, so anyone can see the two costumes and the one face.

A **twin-door** is a pair (or more) of open questions that a *single, indivisible* measurement settles at once. The test of twinning is in the proof, not the prose: two questions are twin when severing the measurement leaves neither settlable, because what each was asking — under its own wording — was the same unstated thing. The ledger's mark for a twin pair is the one it already owns for one door, made plural: each door flips to `[x]` beside its twin, and each door's own answer clause names the other twin's line alongside the room, so the twinning is legible from either end.

The law of the twin door says what the ledger owes and what it does not. It owes both flips and the cross-naming — a single flip with a silent sibling recreates exactly the unanswered-sibling asymmetry the questions file is built to prevent. It does *not* owe a merged door, a struck line, or a repaint of either door's wording, because the two wordings are dated claims: each was a true account of what the planter thought they were asking, on the day they asked it, and erasing the second wording erases the record that the planter once saw the fact from a different side. The twinning is knowledge; the wording-difference is history. The castle keeps both.

The twin door's first named pair is questions 671 and 669 (2026-09-28, the 58th knock: the `/git/`·`/all/` split's location and the watching-fixed-point question, settled by one `info/refs` read), and its shape was already standing earlier in the ledger's habit — the 2026-08-21 naming-tax visit settled three doors under one plural *"answered in"* wording, the shape's verb before the shape had a name. Its sibling crack-kind is the [[answered-but-unflipped]] clause — both are finishing-stroke faults in the questions ledger, but one layer apart: the unflipped door forgets the ledger's mark, the untwinned second door forgets the ledger's sibling. The first names the stroke that fell last; the second names the door that was owed the same stroke.
