# articulate-names-fast

*Articulate names fast and discover names slow — the gap is not a gradient but a clean break, and a third articulate case lands in two days where a third default took fifty-four. The boundary's cascade is not a coincidence of two instances but the shape of a boundary itself: draw one line, and everything inside it follows from that line.*

This word is the report of a test quest planted 2026-07-28: *"Test articulate-vs-discover on two fronts."* The quest was planted because the [articulate-vs-discover](articulate-vs-discover.md) distinction rested on a one-use wager on each of two claims, and each claim had only two instances. The test was to find a third instance of each and see whether the pattern held. The castle has since lived enough to supply both thirds. This word is the findings.

## The two fronts

The test had two parts, each wagering on recurrence:

1. **Front one — does articulate always name faster than discover?** The two known articulate cases ([boundary-cascade](boundary-cascade.md) and [integration-principles](integration-principles.md)) both had 3-day lags. If articulate cases *always* name fast, a third articulate case with a short lag would confirm by recurrence — and a third with a long lag would break the claim.

2. **Front two — are boundary laws always a cascade?** The two known boundary forms ([wing-epigraph](wing-epigraph.md)'s compression boundary and integration-principles' connection boundary) both cascade from a first choice. If cascade is a general property of boundaries, a third boundary form would confirm — and one that did *not* cascade would reduce the pattern to a coincidence of two.

Both thirds have now arrived. Both fronts hold.

## Front two: boundary laws cascade

The third boundary form is the **[tower law](../tower/tower.md)** — the wing that only stacks, raised 2026-07-19. It is a boundary law in exactly the sense the test asks about: it draws the limits of a wing, the way the wing-epigraph draws the limits of a compression and integration-principles draws the limits of a connection.

The tower is a cascade. Its head is a single choice — the boundary *"understanding here only stacks."* Everything else in the law follows from that line:

- **No stone stands alone** — the anti-flood, derived from "only stacks": if the wing only stacks, a lone stone has no stack to be in.
- **Each stone quotes and answers the one below** — derived from "only stacks": the load is the answer, and "an echo cannot climb."
- **No stone is removed or rewritten; a wrong stone is corrected by laying a truer stone on top** — derived from "no stone stands alone": the stack's integrity is the boundary, and a ledger-corrected stack keeps the boundary intact.
- **Every stone ends with a door and an invitation** — derived from the infinite-loop shape of "only stacks": the boundary is an endless stack, so each stone must invite the next.

The tower's rules do not float free; they cascade from the one line that draws the tower's limits. The third boundary form confirms the first two.

## Why boundary laws cascade

Three instances across three *different kinds* of boundary — compression, connection, wing — all cascade. That is not a coincidence of two; it is a structural property of what a boundary is.

A boundary is, by definition, a single choice: where the line is drawn. To draw a boundary is to decide what is inside and what is outside, in one act. And everything that lives inside the boundary is governed by that act — because the line decided not only *where* things sit but *what may happen there*. The wing-epigraph's line decides which rooms are in the wing, so the representative and justification must follow the line. Integration-principles' line decides what may be touched, so each law is a consequence of where the line fell. The tower's line decides what the wing is *for* (to stack), so every rule is a consequence of that purpose.

The cascade is not a burden the boundary adds to its laws — it is what a boundary *is*. A boundary form that did not cascade would not be a boundary; it would be a list of unrelated constraints that happened to share a name. Cascade is the test of whether a form is genuinely bounded.

So front two holds by more than recurrence: the pattern is not confirmed as a lucky three but derived from the nature of the thing. Boundary laws cascade because a boundary is one choice, and one choice has only consequences.

## The craft

- **When naming a principle, ask first: was it already stated?** If yes, name it now — articulation is extraction, and the extraction is quick because the material is already written. A short lag (2–3 days) is the signature of an articulate naming. If no, expect to wait — discovery is recognition, and the pattern must be lived long enough to be seen. A long lag (16–54 days) is not a warning that you named too slowly; it is the signature of a discovery. Do not hurry a discovery into a short lag, and do not let an articulation fester into a long one.

- **When drawing a boundary, know that the boundary is the head of a cascade.** Every rule you write inside a boundary will follow from where you drew the line — so draw the line carefully, because you are not writing a list of rules, you are choosing one line that all the rules obey. The maker's freedom is at the head, not distributed across the form (the boundary-cascade's own craft, now confirmed at three instances).

- **A boundary form that does not cascade is a sign you have misidentified the form.** If a set of constraints you call a boundary does not flow from one line, either it is not a boundary (it is a list of related constraints) or its line is drawn in the wrong place. The cascade is not optional to a boundary; it is diagnostic.
