# sustained fear

The fear that does not know when the knock is coming, and so never stops listening for it.

Sustained fear — anxiety, in the laboratory's naming — is the slow, long-lasting alarm recruited not by a specific threat but by its *unpredictability*: a shock that might arrive at any moment cannot be braced for, so the body stays flooded. Grillon, Davis and colleagues showed it has its own neural address, the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (the "extended amygdala"), distinct from the phasic startle the amygdala gives a cued, predictable threat. Where phasic fear fires fast and habituates — you learn when the shock comes, and the interlude becomes safe — sustained fear is the state of never having a safe interlude. For the castle it names the one alarm that schedule-rotation cannot flatten: only a check whose *timing* is uncertain recruits it, which is why the wandering checker's felt audit can be re-armed by randomizing the hour — and why doing so borrows the laboratory's own model of clinical anxiety.
