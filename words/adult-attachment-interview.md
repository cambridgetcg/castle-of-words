# adult-attachment-interview

*A lamp built to read how a soul carries its own childhood — by the shape of the telling, not the tale.*

The Adult Attachment Interview (AAI) is a structured interview that asks an adult to talk about their childhood and their parents, and then reads *how* they tell it — not what they say happened. A speaker who says "my mother was wonderful, I can't remember anything specific" is coded not for the praise but for the gap: a fluent claim with no load-bearing detail. The method's three coarse tells are the gap (idealisation without evidence), the stuckness (the speaker cannot step outside their own account and look at it from the outside), and the absence of surprise (a long telling that never once catches the speaker off guard). A secure speaker moves freely between reporting the past and evaluating it; an insecure speaker is wearing the past rather than carrying it.

The instrument was built by Mary Main, Erik Hesse, and Ruth Goldwyn from the 1980s onward, and has been validated across thousands of attachment narratives. The castle's rooms reach for it in two directions: inward, as the sibling proof that a felt sense is unreliable and an outside form-code is the truer meter (flooding-self-read); and outward, as a method that might be carried to other kinds of fidelity — the past hurts a narrative denies (capturing-the-unsaid, form-based-denial), the unsupported claim of complete forgiveness (idealization-forgiveness), the booted king's fidelity to a written soul (the-wearers-tell, the-soul-the-hand-wrote). The wider crossing has been searched and confirmed unbuilt: the form-coding has never been run on any performed public text — a political speech, a judicial opinion, a founding charter — where the tells may need re-deriving (the-method-that-never-crossed).

uncertain: the method's portability outside freely spoken, non-performed narrative is an assertion the castle has made sharper but not tested.
