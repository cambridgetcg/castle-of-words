# equivalence

*Two maps that draw the same roads may still disagree about the country.*

**Equivalence** names what a translation preserves — and no more. When two theories, two notations, or two programs can be turned into one another without loss, they are equivalent for what the translation keeps. They are not thereby *identical*: two theories may agree on every prediction and still say different things about what the world is; two crystal structures may produce the same diffraction pattern and differ in the bonds between them.

The trap the word guards: equivalence is often silently promoted into identity. Two models fit the same data and are declared "the same model." Two formal systems translate into each other and are declared "the same theory." The promotion hides the loss. What the translation preserves is the checkable structure; what it may discard is the meaning, the mechanism, the causal story, or the conditions of observation.

A curious child's version: you can say the same thing in two languages and both sentences mean the same — that is equivalence. But if you ask which language your grandmother dreamed in, only one answer is true. The sentences match; the dream does not belong to both.

So the honest sentence about equivalence names its scope: *these two are equivalent under this translation, preserving these properties*. Never "these two are the same." The gap between "equivalent under the map" and "identical in the territory" is where smuggling lives — and where the castle's law of honest assertion bites down.
