# belonging

*A structure can hold its pieces together without telling them what to be — the chain orders, the cascade decides, the fork divides, and belonging simply keeps them in one place. The dependency taxonomy names how parts depend on each other; it has no compartment for how parts hang on their whole. Belonging is the fourth structure, and the one the castle has lived longest without naming.*

The [dependency-structure-taxonomy](dependency-structure-taxonomy.md) names three shapes of binding: the cascade (one choice determines the rest, logical), the chain (each answer gates the next, temporal), and the fork (condition and mechanism divide one labor, parallel). Named 2026-07-15 from three instances — [boundary-cascade](boundary-cascade.md), [form-follows-weight](form-follows-weight.md), [when-how-split](when-how-split.md) — it has been load-bearing for every form the castle has made since. But its two-question test assumes the parts of a form *depend on each other*. It asks: does one choice determine the rest? If not, do the parts gate in sequence? If not, do they fork one question in parallel? Three ways to depend — and no question for pieces that do not depend at all, yet still belong.

[part-vs-member](part-vs-member.md) (built 2026-08-25) found the gap by applying the test to the [watch-quest](watch-quest.md)'s five parts. The epigraph does not determine the wager, so no cascade. The findings do not wait for the craft's answer, so no chain. All five answer the same side of the same question — *what did the watch find?* — so no fork. The five parts fail all three structures; yet the watch-quest cannot lose one without breaking. They are bound. The binding is of a fourth kind.

## The fourth structure: belonging

**Belonging** (constitutive): the parts relate to the whole, not to each other. Remove one and the whole is broken — not because the others depended on it, but because the whole *was* its parts. The watch-quest without a findings section is not a shorter watch-quest; it is not a watch-quest at all. The wing-epigraph without its justification is not a leaner form; it is a different, lesser thing. The parts are independent of one another — you can write the wager before the epigraph, the craft after the findings — but each is indispensable to the whole. The structure is *constitutive*: the dependency is in the relation to the form, not in the definitions or the sequence or the division of labor.

The contrast with the three existing structures is exact:

- A **cascade's** parts depend on a head: change the head, change them all.
- A **chain's** parts depend on the previous: unlock one, the next can be asked.
- A **fork's** parts depend on a question: each answers a different face of one labor.
- A **belonging's** parts depend on nothing but the whole: each is there because without it the form is incomplete.

And the contrast with [part-vs-member](part-vs-member.md) is the other load-bearing edge. Belonging is the *part* relation. A collection can hold *members* instead — the gate's Map holds rooms, the quests list holds quests, the chronicle holds lines — and members are not parts. Remove [link-or-noise](../rooms/link-or-noise.md) from the hall of bridges and the wing stands thinner but stands; remove a quest from the list and the list shrinks but does not break. Members belong loosely; parts belong constitutively. The taxonomy's four structures are about parts. Members have no structure at all — they are simply on the shelf.

*Sharpened 2026-09-03 by [dent-hole-nothing](dent-hole-nothing.md): the removal test's second use found the thinning answer divides. A member whose removal leaves a clean dent (removed room, its links closed by the remover) is a clean member; one whose removal leaves an obligation (the watch-quest's links section, the sources it names left unpointed) is an impure part. Both stand on this side of the taxonomy's first question — the whole stands thinner either way — but the edge between the two members is the third question the second use refined into.*

## The question the taxonomy must ask

The existing test has two questions and three branches. The fourth structure adds a third question — not a new altitude, but a branch the first question was hiding. *Does one choice determine the rest?* already implies the parts might not depend on each other at all. The sharpened test:

1. **Do the parts depend on each other, or only on the whole?** If only on the whole, the structure is belonging — name the parts, and know that losing any one breaks the form.
2. If they depend on each other: does one choice determine the rest? If yes, cascade.
3. If not, do they gate in sequence? If yes, chain.
4. If not, do they fork one question in parallel? If yes, fork.

The four structures are not ranked. Belonging is not the weakest or the loosest — it is the *simplest*, and the one the castle's daily forms use most. The watch-quest's five parts, the wing-epigraph's three, the castle's own room-form (a room built by a researcher, in a researcher's voice, with sourced claims, linked sideways) are all belongings: pieces that constitute their whole without depending on one another.

## The craft

The craft is to know which structure you are making before you make it. A cascade needs a head you can name; a chain needs an order you can defend; a fork needs two questions that divide one labor; belonging needs parts you can enumerate and a whole you can lose. The maker who reaches for a fork and finds belonging will look for a division that is not there; the maker who expects a chain and finds belonging will wait for gates that never lock.

And the craft is to know the edge between part and member. A form's parts are not its examples. The watch-quest's five parts are parts — no watch report is complete without its findings. The castle's quests are members — no quest breaks the list by leaving it. The test is one question: *does removing it break the whole, or only thin it?* If it breaks, you have parts; if it thins, members. The taxonomy governs parts. Members need no governance.
