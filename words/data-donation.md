# data-donation

A stranger hands the researcher their own diary — the pages already written, the asking happens after.

In the castle's screen-time wing, data donation is the method where a platform user requests their own usage archive (under GDPR's data-portability right or the platform's download tool) and gives it to a study — so the screen-time hours and click logs are the platform's own records, not the participant's recollection. It answers the known gap in [goldilocks-leaves-home](../rooms/goldilocks-leaves-home.md) and [[the-unarchived-ladder]]: every Goldilocks crossing so far has measured time online by asking the user to remember it, and retrospective self-report of screen use correlates with logged use only weakly. The Global Kids Online survey has the well-being ladder but recalled hours; a donation study would have logged hours, and the two together are the clean quadratic test that stands unbuilt.

What the method has actually done (read 2026-08-28, OpenAlex): donation has crossed into the Global South once — Amin, Müller & Rieger 2023 collected Screen Time / Digital Wellbeing screenshots from 427 Pakistani citizens with direct well-being measures (depression, loneliness, life satisfaction), found donated use negatively associated with well-being while self-report in the same sample showed nothing, and declared the method "viable in Pakistan" (Digital Health, doi:10.1177/20552076231186075). Its nearest Western neighbour is the Appnome study: 374 Swiss adolescents, two weeks of donated screenshots against daily hedonic and eudaimonic well-being, effects small or null (Scientific Reports 2024, doi:10.1038/s41598-024-81665-8). But no donation study anywhere has yet fitted the quadratic the Goldilocks hypothesis is made of — every passport page so far carries a straight line. Feasibility with adolescents is established too: a UK/NL 2-week trial (N=358, aged 13–18) saw 78.9% donate Instagram data and 65.8% TikTok, with six practical recommendations (Annals of the NYAS 2025, doi:10.1111/nyas.70140).
