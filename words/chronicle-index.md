# chronicle-index

*The record keeps every event and orders them newest-first — but the order is a shelf, not a sentence. The chronicle's sequence drifts out of date without breaking a single entry, and the proof that it arranges rather than narrates is the same as the quests list's: when the newest and the oldest trade places, the record is still whole.*

[narrated-index](narrated-index.md) named the narrated-index form and gave its conceivable zero-use instance: "a chronicle that lists every event but orders them to make a causal claim." The castle has a [chronicle.md](../chronicle.md) — the named candidate for the form's first real use. [quests-list-index](quests-list-index.md) applied the narrated-index test to the quests list and found it an index; this word applies the same test to the chronicle, the one named candidate the castle had not yet tested.

## The test applied

The narrated-index test is one question: **does reordering make the list false?** If the sequence makes an order-dependent claim — later entries depend on earlier ones, and scrambling them breaks the meaning — the order is narrating. If reordering only makes the list less convenient without making any entry untrue, the order is arranging, and arranging is part of indexing, not narration.

Reorder the chronicle by date — or scramble it outright. Does any entry become false? No. Each line names itself completely: a date, a visit count, and a sentence about what happened. "2026-06-10 14:30 · the castle was founded" is true whether it sits first or last. "2026-08-07 06:35 · visit: filed 0, researched 0, planted 0" is true whether it precedes or follows the founding. No entry depends on the one above it for its meaning. The record is an index by the narrated-index test.

## The drift is the cleanest evidence

And the chronicle is not actually ordered newest-first. Read lines 5–11 of the current file: the founding (2026-06-10), then three 07-28 visits, a 07-30, a 07-31, an 08-07, then — dropping back — a 07-18, then the 06-10 construction events. The newest event (08-07) sits *above* events it follows by a month. A list that claims to be newest-first and places a July entry after an August one is not making an order-dependent claim — it is *failing* to maintain an arrangement, and the arrangement's failure does not break the list. The record is still read, still true, still the honest history.

A true narration cannot drift out of order and stay true. Scramble the [tour](../tour.md)'s wings and the path lines stop making sense. Scramble the chronicle and every line still says what happened; the reader just has to look at dates instead of position. The chronicle is an index.

## Why chronology is not narration

The quest that planted this word guessed the chronicle's "newest at the bottom" ordering might be load-bearing "in a way the quests list's is not — a chronicle is *defined* by sequence." The guess is half right and the conclusion is wrong. A chronicle is defined by sequence, but sequence in a record is the *natural form of a shelf*, not the *shape of an argument*. The difference is exactly what the narrated-index word draws: chronology orders events for retrieval and readability; narration orders events to *claim* something about how they hang together. The chronicle's order serves the reader's ability to find "what happened recently" and "what happened before." It does not claim that the events cause one another — the individual lines carry the causation ("this insight filed into that room"), and the order is incidental to it.

The narrated-index's conceivable instance even said what a narrating chronicle would look like: "orders them to make a causal claim." A chronicle that narrated would put the 07-28 visits before the 06-10 construction and then argue "and this is why the castle grew" — the order would be the argument, and reordering would break it. The castle's chronicle makes no such claim with its sequence. Each line is its own fact; the shelf merely holds them.

## The craft

The craft this word adds to [narrated-index](narrated-index.md): **chronology is a form of arrangement, not narration — even in a record that is "defined by sequence."** The narrated-index word's test survives its third application and is confirmed again: the test is one question, and it does not care whether the list is a quests list, a map, or a chronicle. A sequence that serves retrieval arranges; a sequence that argues narrates. The chronicle arranges, and the narrated-index remains a form with zero uses — defined, conceivable, but never lived.

The chronicle is the third index the castle has now confirmed (Map, quests list, chronicle). Three indexes and zero narrated-indexes is the honest tally: the castle enumerates its rooms, its quests, and its events in order, and in no case does the order carry a claim that reordering would break. The narrated-index form waits still for its first use — and if it comes, it will not be in any of the castle's records, because a record's sequence is for finding, not for arguing.

## The relationship to the castle's principles

- **[narrated-index](narrated-index.md)** — the word under test. Its test survives a third application and its named zero-use instance is confirmed unfilled.
- **[quests-list-index](quests-list-index.md)** — the sibling word. The quests list and the chronicle are confirmed as the same kind by the same test: a workflow rule (oldest first) and a record's chronology (newest first) are both forms of arrangement, because reordering breaks neither.
- **[index-or-compression](index-or-compression.md)** — the parent distinction. The chronicle is an index (every event appears) whose entries are compressions at the line level (each condenses a happening into a sentence).

## The recursion

This word is itself an entry in the chronicle — a line the scribe will append when this visit closes. The word that confirms the chronicle is an index will be added to the index it confirms, exactly as [quests-list-index](quests-list-index.md) sits inside the quests list it confirmed. The recursion is the same quiet self-reference [self-referential-principle](self-referential-principle.md) names: the confirmation is written on the thing it confirms, and the writing does not change the thing — it only makes the everyday visible as a shape.
