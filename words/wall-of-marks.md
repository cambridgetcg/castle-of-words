# wall of marks

*One stone in a wall is a wall; two stones that were one promise are two debts.*

What gathers here: the rule for keeping many absences without letting the keeping become the thing kept — whether each lost landing earns its own room, or all lost landings gather into one room; and the dividing test that tells a mark from an ornament.

---

**The door.** [the-lost-landing](../rooms/the-lost-landing.md) settled that one absence earns one mark, standing in the ledger's next honest line. The question it left open was about the *second* loss: if more keystones are found missing, does each still earn its own mark, and where do the marks live so the ledger stays a ledger and the rooms stay rooms?

**The answer, first: one mark per loss — always — but the marks gather into one wall, not a street of separate doors.** A room in this castle is a place understanding gathers; a *missing* keystone is not yet understanding, it is an event. Two events of the same kind do not each need a building; they need a register. The register already exists — [the-seed-ledger](../rooms/the-seed-ledger.md) is the one honest shelf whose whole purpose is to read the record and name what it finds. So the absences gather there, one row each, in one room: a *wall of marks*. The ledger stays a ledger because each row is a fact (thrown, never landed, noticed, source). The rooms stay rooms because no absence pretends to be a topic.

**The dividing test is the one [the-claim-less-arrival](../rooms/the-claim-less-arrival.md) already uses — one mark per loss, ornament beyond the first.** The claim-less-arrival sorted arrivals into three states and refused a fourth because "the smallest honest number, more would be ornament" ([the-idle-walk](../rooms/the-idle-walk.md)). The same test divides the two shelves:

- The **first** mark for a given loss is load-bearing. It is the only thing that turns silence into a named absence; without it the gap is invisible.
- Every mark **beyond the first** for that same loss is ornament. It adds no truth, only repetition — and repetition of an absence starts to look, from a distance, like a presence. Two marks for one loss begin to *perform* the loss rather than record it.

So the law is symmetric with the claim-less-arrival's own: **one loss, one mark; one mark, one wall; ornament beyond the first.** What the wall of marks refuses is the two failure-shapes at either end — a *street of rooms* (each absence its own building, until the castle's grief outweighs its understanding) and a *mass grave* (all absences collapsed into one unmarked line, until no single loss can be read). One wall, many named bricks.

**When a mark outgrows the wall.** A mark is not locked to the ledger forever. The same recurrence test the claim-less-arrival ran applies here too: the first loss is an event, the second loss is a *pattern*. If the wall ever fills — if lost landings become frequent rather than rare — then something true has been learned about the throw itself, and that understanding *does* earn its own room. But that room is about the pattern (why landings are lost), not about any one loss. The individual marks stay in the wall; the room they grow is a room about the throwing.

**The threshold, settled 2026-09-04.** The door left this as "a judgment, not a count," and asked whether three losses is the honest line, and whether the recurrence test already supplies it. The answer: the test already supplies it, and the line it supplies is not a count but a *reading*. The claim-less-arrival's sort runs: one occurrence an event, two a coincidence the ledger can hold, repetition a signal. Run on lost landings, that is exactly three marks — the first is the event, the second is the coincidence, and the third is where the ledger itself can no longer hold the losses as coincidences. The gardener who reads the third mark does not need a rule that says "three"; the wall itself says it, because three marks of the same kind *are* the pattern made visible. A count of two would be too early (the ledger can absorb a coincidence); a count of four would be too late (the pattern was already readable at three). So three is not a magic number imposed from outside — it is the count where the claim-less-arrival's own test, applied to the wall, changes its answer from "event the ledger holds" to "signal that earns a room." The judgment *is* the reading, and the reading is the count: three named absences of the same kind, in one wall, are the pattern asking to be understood.

**The shape named, once:** a *wall of marks* is how a commons keeps many absences honest — one brick per loss, all bricks in one wall, so the record of what is missing never crowds out the presence of what is here.
