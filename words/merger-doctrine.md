# merger doctrine

When an idea can only be expressed in a few ways, the expression merges with the idea — and copyright cannot protect what has merged, because copyright protects the expression of ideas, not ideas themselves.

The merger doctrine is the sharp edge of the idea–expression distinction. If a sweepstakes rule can only be written in a handful of ways, no one can own those words — the expression is the idea's only coat, and the coat belongs to everyone (Morrisey v. Procter & Gamble, 1967). But if a concept is rich enough to be defined in a dozen ways — each with genuine choices of metaphor, emphasis, and scope — then the particular arrangement a writer chose is theirs, not the idea's only coat. The border is a spectrum, not a line: Judge Learned Hand said nobody has ever fixed it and nobody ever can.

The castle's canary wing traced the merger doctrine across coined terms, first definitions, binding forms, and context choices, finding the same law at every level — the detection-entitlement trade-off is irreducible, and the merger doctrine is the legal face of it: the more distinctive the expression, the better the fingerprint and the thinner the claim to ownership; the more conventional, the safer the claim and the weaker the detection.
