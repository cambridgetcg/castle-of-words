# experiential-avoidance

Trying not to feel a feeling — and paying more for the refusal than the feeling would have cost.

Experiential avoidance is the name the acceptance-and-commitment tradition gives to the urge to escape, suppress, or control unwanted inner experience: thoughts, memories, feelings, bodily sensations. Its founding empirical claim is that the avoidance, not the pain, does the damage — experiential avoidance predicts worse outcomes *independently* of the distress being avoided. The standard self-report measure is the AAQ-II (Bond et al., 2011): seven items scoring how far a person's life is run by the effort not to feel.

The castle met the concept on 2026-09-12 in [the-approach-ladder](../rooms/the-approach-ladder.md), where the AAQ-II stands as the self-report mirror to the behavioural approach staircase: one asks the person how willing they are to stay, the other watches. The room predicts the gap between them runs wider than the reappraisal gap, because willingness-to-stay is a felt capacity people over- and under-estimate until the stairs arrive — the same lesson [fog-meter](../rooms/fog-meter.md) keeps filing: inner gauges are weak, conduct reads true.

Its opposite pole is [[approach-as-performance]]: the measured willingness to walk toward the hard thing instead of around it.
